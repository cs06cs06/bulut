# 15 Things From the 70s You Don't See Anymore (#1 Was Everywhere)

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Dad on the roof, twisting the antenna, Mom yelling, Better? Worse? Here are fifteen seventies things that vanished. And number one? It sat on every coffee table, desk and dashboard. Today it's nearly gone, yet one place is still required by law to have one.

## #14 The Phone Booth on the Corner

**s02 · photos**

Remember the phone booth? A folding glass door squeaked shut behind you. A thick phone book dangled on a chain. You dropped your coins and heard them ding all the way down. In Canada, payphones took nickels, dimes and quarters. And every kid ran a finger through the coin-return slot, just in case. Sometimes a forgotten dime was waiting. Back then, it was the easiest way to call home from the road. Even in two thousand, there were still about two point two million payphones in the United States. Seven years later, fewer than half remained. That little glass box was our lifeline.

## #13 Avocado Green and Harvest Gold Kitchens

**s03 · photos**

Walk into a new kitchen in the seventies and you saw green. Avocado green. Or harvest gold. Or a coppery brown. The fridge matched the stove. The stove matched the dishwasher. Sometimes the wall phone and the flour canisters matched too. Mom picked that color the way Dad picked a car, and we expected it to last for decades. Today, appliances come in white, black or stainless steel, and an avocado fridge would make a real estate agent wince. But back then? That kitchen was the height of modern living. And honestly, it hid fingerprints beautifully.

## #12 Flashcubes on Christmas Morning

**s04 · photos**

Christmas morning. Everybody squeezed onto the sofa. Dad held up the camera with a little flashcube on top. Pop! A blinding flash, a whiff of hot plastic, and the cube turned for the next shot. Each one held four tiny flashbulbs, one on every side. Once they fired, the bulbs went cloudy blue, and out the cube went. Everyone in the picture had red eyes and saw spots for a minute afterward. We didn't mind. That was just how Christmas looked. Flashcubes were still on store shelves around nineteen seventy-seven. Now your phone flashes silently, again and again. Where's the fun in that?

## #11 The Giant Wooden Console Stereo

**s05 · photos**

In the living room stood a long walnut cabinet, polished till it gleamed. The family stereo. Lift the lid, and there was a record player, an AM and FM radio, and in some homes, even an eight-track. The speakers hid behind fabric at each end. On top sat family photos, a candy dish, maybe a plant. And you did not touch Dad's records without asking. It was furniture first and music second, and it weighed about as much as the piano. Today, all that music fits in a speaker the size of a coffee cup. It just doesn't smell like lemon polish.

## #10 The Kitchen Wall Intercom

**s06 · photos**

Some lucky homes had a speaker panel right on the kitchen wall, with little buttons for talk and listen. Mom pressed one, and her voice crackled into every bedroom. Supper's ready! No more shouting up the stairs. Some families even piped the radio through the whole house, so the music followed you from room to room. We thought it was the future. Kids used it to spy on their sisters, naturally. Today, families text each other from the next room. And that old intercom panel? Usually painted over, a mystery box nobody remembers how to use.

## #9 Wall-to-Wall Shag Carpet

**s07 · photos**

Then there was the carpet. Shag. Long, furry yarns, an inch and a half to three inches deep, in orange, gold or avocado green. It was made from the new synthetic fibers, nylon and acrylic, and it ran wall to wall. In some houses it even crept into the bathroom. Kids sprawled on it in front of the TV, and it felt wonderful under bare feet. But drop a crayon, a bobby pin or a dime, and good luck. Today we have hard floors and low carpet that's easy to clean. Shag was cozy and warm. And whatever fell into it belonged to the carpet now. If that clink of glass brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the seventies together.

## #8 The Milkman at Dawn

**s08 · photos**

Before the sun came up, you heard it. A gentle clink of glass on the porch. The milkman. He wore a white uniform and left cold bottles in an insulated box by the door. Need more? You rolled a little note into the empty bottle. Two homogenized, one cream. In nineteen fifty-four, more than half of the milk sold in America came to the door like that. By nineteen seventy-five, it was down to about seven percent. Supermarkets and big refrigerators won. But nobody ever forgot that clink. It was the sound of a good morning.

## #7 Sharing a Party Line

**s09 · photos**

Pick up the phone, and someone was already talking. Your neighbor. On a party line, several houses shared one telephone line. Each house had its own ring, two shorts, or one long and one short, and you learned to count. In the middle of the last century, a great many families in the United States and Canada shared a line like this, because a private line cost more. Out in the country, everybody knew everybody's business. A soft click meant someone was listening in. The polite thing was to say, I need the line, please. By the late eighties, most were gone.

## #6 The Neighbors' Vacation Slide Show

**s10 · photos**

The neighbors got back from the Grand Canyon, and you knew what that meant. Slide night. The lights went down. The projector fan hummed. The round tray clacked forward, one slide at a time. Here's the canyon. Here's the canyon again. Here's Aunt Ruth squinting at the canyon. And at least once, a slide popped up upside down, and everybody laughed. Those round-tray projectors came out in nineteen sixty-two, and by the seventies, sitting through a friend's vacation slides was simply good manners. Today we swipe through photos in ten seconds. Faster, sure. But nobody passes the popcorn.

## #5 The TV Signed Off at Night

**s11 · photos**

Late at night, the television actually went to bed. After the last show, the station played the national anthem over a waving flag. In Canada, it was O Canada, and some stations played God Save the Queen first. Then came the test pattern, that strange target of circles and lines, and a long, steady tone. Then just snow and static. If you'd dozed off on the couch, that hiss was what woke you, bathed in blue light. Stations kept that custom for decades. Today TV never sleeps. Back then, even the TV knew when it was bedtime.

## #4 The Wood-Paneled Basement Rec Room

**s12 · photos**

Down the basement stairs, past the furnace, was the rec room. Dark wood paneling on every wall. A plaid couch that had retired from upstairs. A ping-pong table, a record player, maybe a little bar Dad built himself. It always smelled a bit damp, no matter what. This was where the teenagers went, where birthday parties happened, and where a lot of us heard our first slow song. Parents stayed upstairs, mostly. Today it's bright drywall and open floor plans. But that paneled room, with its one buzzing light, was the best room in the house.

## #3 Dad on the Roof With the Antenna

**s13 · photos**

Saturday afternoon. The picture was fuzzy again. So Dad climbed onto the roof and grabbed the antenna mast. Someone stood at the window shouting, Better? Worse? No, go back! Inside, the rabbit ears on top of the set wore little tinfoil flags, just for luck. Some houses had a rotor box that turned the antenna with a dial, but most of us had Dad. In nineteen seventy-eight, only about seven and a half percent of American homes had cable. The rest of us pulled the signal right out of the sky. Today, those antennas are mostly gone. So are the arguments on the lawn.

## #2 The Eight-Track in the Dashboard

**s14 · photos**

Ka-chunk. You pushed a chunky plastic cartridge into the player under the dashboard, and music filled the car. The eight-track. It arrived in nineteen sixty-five as an option in some new cars, and it played in an endless loop, about eighty minutes, then right back to the start. No rewinding. And right in the middle of your favorite song, a loud click as it switched tracks. Shoeboxes of tapes rattled on the floor of the station wagon. Sales peaked in nineteen seventy-eight. By nineteen eighty-three, most stores had dropped them. Funny thing, it was mostly a North American love affair. The rest of the world barely knew it.

## #1 The Ashtray Was Everywhere

**s15 · photos**

And number one. The ashtray. Heavy amber glass on the coffee table. A big one on every office desk. A pull-out tray in the car dashboard, right next to the lighter. Even little ashtrays in airplane armrests. In the mid-sixties, more than forty-two percent of American adults smoked, and ashtrays seemed to be on every table and desk. We even brought souvenir ones home from vacation. The seventies brought the first no-smoking sections, and by two thousand, smoking was banned on nearly all American flights. And yet, here's the twist. Airplane lavatories are still required to have an ashtray today. Just in case somebody breaks the rules.
