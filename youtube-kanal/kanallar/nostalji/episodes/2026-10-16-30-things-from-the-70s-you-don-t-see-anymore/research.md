# Research: 15 Things From the 70s You Don't See Anymore

> **Read this first.** My search and fetch tools hit their usage limit partway through, so I only checked part of this list. Any fact with a source address in brackets was confirmed this session. Anything marked **[unverified]** comes from my general knowledge and wasn't checked against a source. The script should either check those facts first or say them only in vague terms. The three items with the most checked facts are #1 (8-track), #9 (milkman) and #4 (ashtrays). Brand names are left out wherever possible.

## Candidate items

1. **8-track tape player in the car and the den** — A chunky plastic cartridge slid in with a "ka-chunk." The music cut out in the middle of a song with a loud click when it switched tracks. The tape played in an endless loop and couldn't be rewound. People had shoeboxes of tapes on the floor of the car. | The format was introduced in 1965. On Sept 15, 1965, a carmaker offered 8-track players as an option in some of its sports and luxury models, and record labels followed. A tape ran about 80 minutes before starting over from the beginning. US sales peaked in 1978, the tapes were gone from most stores by 1983, and mail-order record clubs offered some titles until late 1988. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-track_cartridge; bestclassicbands.com/8-track-tapes-introduction-9-15-155/) | Today: cassettes took over around 1983–91, then CDs and streaming. | US/Canada: The format was "most popular in the US and Canada" and nearly unknown in most other countries (Wikipedia). | Image: A man's hand pushing a cartridge into an under-dash player in a wood-grain-trimmed sedan, late-afternoon highway light, tapes scattered on a vinyl bench seat.

2. **Phone booths and payphones on every corner** — There was a folding glass door, a phone book hanging on a chain, the ding of coins dropping, and a coin-return slot that kids always checked. | In 2000 there were still 2.2 million payphones across the US. By 2007 only 872,000 were left, as cell phones spread. In the early 1900s a local call cost first ten cents, then five. (ethw.org/Public_Telephones; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LinkNYC) The usual 1970s local call was a dime **[unverified, and it varied by city]**. | Today: Cell phones made them pointless, and most were taken out in the 2000s. | US/Canada: Canadian payphones from the 1970s–80s, made in Canada, accepted 5¢, 10¢ and 25¢ coins. Early ones had rotary dials and later ones had keypads. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nortel_payphones) | Image: A teenager in a bell-bottom jacket inside a lit booth at dusk, coins lined up on the ledge, a gas station sign glowing behind.

3. **Rooftop TV antenna (and rabbit ears on top of the set)** — Dad was up on the roof turning the mast while someone shouted "Better? Worse?" through the window. There was tinfoil on the rabbit ears, and some houses had a rotor control box on top of the set. | Only 6.4% of US households had cable in 1968, and still only 7.5% in 1978. That figure was 52.8% in 1988 and 62.4% in 1994. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_television) Cable began in 1948 as community antennas on hilltops for towns where terrain blocked the signal. | Today: Cable, then satellite and streaming. Antennas are a niche comeback. | US/Canada: Canada adopted cable faster in the 1970s, especially near the border, to pick up US stations **[unverified]**. | Image: A father on a shingled roof holding the antenna mast, a mother and kids looking up from the lawn, a suburban street in the background.

4. **Ashtrays everywhere** — Heavy glass or amber ashtrays sat on coffee tables, office desks, restaurant tables and car dashboards (with pull-out trays and lighters). Armrest ashtrays on airplanes, the stale smell of smoke, souvenir ashtrays from vacations. | In the mid-1960s more than 42% of US adults smoked, and ashtrays "seemed to be on every table and desk." The 1970s brought the first no-smoking sections on airplanes and in restaurants. (timesargus.com) The US banned smoking on flights under two hours in 1988 and on nearly all flights by 2000. Airplane lavatories are still required to have ashtrays today. (simpleflying.com) | Today: Smoke-free laws, and the US adult smoking rate is down to roughly 18% according to that 2014 article. | US/Canada: In 1960s Canada you could smoke in offices, hospital waiting rooms and university classrooms (cigstore.ca). That is a retailer's site, so treat it as color, not authority. | Image: An office desk with a typewriter, a rotary phone and a full glass ashtray, smoke curling up in window light.

5. **Wall-to-wall shag carpet** — Long, furry pile in orange, avocado or gold, sometimes even in bathrooms. Some homes had a special shag rake to fluff it up **[the rake is unverified]**. | Shag is high-pile carpet with yarns 1½ to 3 inches long, popular in the 1960s–70s. It was made from new synthetic fibers like nylon and acrylic, and avocado and harvest gold were signature colors. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shag_(fabric); cheapism.com) | Today: Low-pile carpet and hard floors won out. | US/Canada: No difference. | Image: Barefoot kids lying on orange shag in front of a console TV, wood-paneled wall, warm lamp light.

6. **Avocado-green and harvest-gold appliances** — Matching fridge, stove and dishwasher, often with a phone and canisters in the same color. | Refrigerators, ovens, dishwashers and small appliances came in avocado green, harvest gold and coppertone. White, black and stainless steel later took over. (cheapism.com, the 14-item article) | Today: Neutral colors. | Image: A mother at an avocado stove in a kitchen with gold countertops, a wall phone with a long curly cord.

7. **Rotary dial phone (rented from the phone company)** — The whirr and click of the dial. Waiting for the 0 to spin all the way back. One heavy phone on the kitchen wall, with a cord stretched into the pantry for privacy. | The rotary dial went into general use in most homes by the 1950s. Touch-Tone dialing arrived on Nov 18, 1963, in Carnegie and Greensburg, Pennsylvania. It took years to spread, partly because phone companies rented out the equipment. (clickamericana.com; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-button_telephone) | Today: Cell phones, and home landlines are disappearing. | US/Canada: Renting phones was the norm in both countries **[details unverified]**. | Image: A teenage girl sitting on the kitchen floor, the curly cord stretched taut from the wall phone, dialing with one finger.

8. **Party lines** — Several houses shared one line, each with its own ring pattern. Neighbors listened in, and a polite "I need the line, please" was common. | In the mid-20th century most Bell System customers in the US and Canada were on party lines, which were cheaper than a private line. They had been removed in most places by the late 1980s (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_line_(telephony)). Some rural West Virginia party lines lasted until 1989 (holidaypaths.com, a weak source). | Today: Private lines and then cell phones. | US/Canada: Common in both, especially in rural areas. | Image: A farm kitchen with a woman holding a wall phone and covering the mouthpiece, a curious look on her face.

9. **The milkman** — Glass bottles clinking on the porch at dawn, an insulated milk box by the door, and a note left in the empty bottle ("2 homo, 1 cream"). | In 1954 home delivery was 54% of US milk sales. It fell to 19.3% by 1971, 6.9% by 1975 and about 0.4% of consumers by 2005. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_delivery; grocerydive.com cites USDA as about 30% in 1963 and 7% in 1975) | Today: Supermarkets and home refrigerators took over, with small boutique delivery revivals. | US/Canada: Milk in plastic bags plus a pitcher is a Canadian thing (Ontario, Quebec, the Maritimes) **[unverified]**. | Image: A milkman in a white uniform setting bottles in a porch box at sunrise, a step-van at the curb.

10. **Flashcubes** — A little four-sided cube that turned after every shot. There was a "pop," a blinding flash, a whiff of hot plastic, and the used bulbs went cloudy blue. Everyone got red eyes. | The cube held four disposable flashbulbs, each in its own reflector, and turned on top of the camera. It was developed by a lighting company and adopted for the popular 1960s pocket cameras (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_(photography)). It was still sold around 1977, and stray glass from a bulb could cut someone (slate.com). | Today: Built-in electronic flash, then phone cameras. | Image: A family on a Christmas morning sofa squinting into a flash, wrapping paper on the floor.

11. **Vacation slide shows on a living-room screen** — The neighbors came over, the lights went down, the projector fan hummed, the tray clacked, and one slide always showed up upside down. | The round-tray slide projector came out in 1962 (obsoletemedia.org; campaignlive.co.uk). In the 1970s, a writer recalls, you were expected to invite returning vacationers over for their slide show (yahoo.com). | Today: Phone photos and digital frames. | Image: A dark living room, a pull-up screen showing a canyon slide, faces lit by the projector beam.

12. **The TV signed off for the night** — After the late show came the anthem and a waving flag, then a test pattern and a steady tone, then snow and static. | Stations played the anthem as a custom. The only US legal requirement was the station's call sign (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign-on_and_sign-off). In one North Carolina market, sign-offs ended around the late 1980s or early 1990s (journalnow.com). That is one market only, so don't generalize. | Today: TV runs 24 hours. | US/Canada: Canadian stations played "O Canada," and some also played "God Save the Queen" first (Wikipedia). | Image: A kid asleep on the couch in blue TV light, the test pattern on screen.

13. **Giant wooden console stereo** — A long walnut cabinet with a lift-up lid. Inside were a record player, an AM/FM radio and sometimes an 8-track. Family photos sat on top. | Source: cheapism.com (14-item article). | Today: Bluetooth speakers. | Image: A teenager kneeling at an open console with a record in hand, an album sleeve on the shag.

14. **The TV set as a piece of furniture** — A floor-model TV in a wooden cabinet, with knickknacks, a plant and photos on top, and a dial you got up to turn. | 1970s TVs were "furniture," a place to put knickknacks (yahoo.com). | Today: Flat screens on the wall. | Image: A mother dusting the top of a cabinet TV covered in framed school photos.

15. **Built-in wall intercoms** — A speaker panel in the kitchen with "Talk/Listen" buttons. Mom called the kids for supper, and some piped the radio through the whole house. | Source: cheapism.com (14-item article). | Today: Cell phones and texting replaced them. | Image: A mother pressing a wall intercom button, steam rising from pots behind her.

16. **Typewriter and correction fluid** — The clack of keys, the bell at the end of a line, the return lever, carbon paper, and white correction fluid drying on the mistake. | The memory is in yahoo.com. Specific facts are **[unverified]**. | Today: Computers. | Image: A woman at a kitchen table typing a letter with a little bottle of correction fluid beside her.

17. **Five-and-dime photo booth** — A curtain, a spinning stool, four black-and-white frames for a quarter, and the strip came out damp. | The quarter price is one writer's memory (59streetbridge.com), so treat it as anecdote. | Today: Rare; selfies replaced them. | Image: Two teens squeezed into a booth making faces, a lunch counter visible outside.

18. **Macramé plant hangers** — Knotted jute rope holding spider plants in front of every window. | Source: cheapism.com (14-item article). It has made partial comebacks. | Image: A sunny window with three hanging plants and a woman knotting a new hanger.

19. **Wood-paneled walls and the basement rec room** — Dark paneling, a bar, a ping-pong table, a slightly damp smell, and teen parties. | Paneling is in cheapism.com; rec rooms are in yahoo.com. | Today: Drywall, painted walls, open floor plans. | Image: A paneled basement with teens around a record player and a plaid couch.

20. **Smoked-glass mushroom lamps and amber light** — Dome shades in amber or smoked glass with a warm, dim glow. | Source: cheapism.com (15-item article). | Image: An amber lamp beside a brown corduroy sofa.

21. **Conversation pits and sunken living rooms** — Built-in seating around a lowered floor. These were rare, so the item may feel less universal to viewers. | Source: cheapism.com (15-item article). | Image: Friends in a sunken pit with fondue on the coffee table.

22. **Vinyl tablecloths** — Wipe-clean, slightly sticky in summer, with checks or flowers. | Mentioned in yahoo.com; no other facts. | Image: A picnic table with a vinyl cloth and a pitcher of drink mix.

23. **Shoe-store foot X-ray machines** *(mostly 1950s; by the 70s it's a memory)* — Kids peeked at their own toe bones through the viewer. | It exposed you to radiation (59streetbridge.com, a humor blog). The years the machines were phased out and the state bans are **[unverified]**. Use only as a "remember when" aside. | Image: A boy looking into a wooden cabinet viewer while a clerk watches.

24. **Eaton's catalogue (Canada) / the big holiday wish-book catalogue (US)** — Kids circled toys in pen, and the catalogue got a second life in the outhouse and as a hockey shin pad. | The Eaton's catalogue was discontinued in 1976 **[unverified]**. The US department-store catalogue ran until about 1993 **[unverified]**. | Today: Online shopping. | US/Canada: A strong Canada item. | Image: Two kids on a braided rug flipping through a thick catalogue by a Christmas tree.

25. **Cigarette commercials on TV and radio** — Jingles and cartoon characters selling cigarettes on TV. | In the mid-1960s, athletes and cartoon characters endorsed cigarettes in TV ads (timesargus.com). The US broadcast ad ban starting Jan 2, 1971, is **[unverified]**, and so is Canada's voluntary industry withdrawal of broadcast ads in the early 1970s. Do not name the people involved. | Image: A family watching TV, with a softly lit ad on the screen (no readable brand).

26. **Paper $1 and $2 bills in Canada / metric changeover** — Canadians paid with paper ones and twos, then saw roadside speed signs switch to km/h. | The loonie arrived in 1987 and the toonie in 1996 **[unverified]**. Road signs switched to metric around 1977 **[unverified]**. | Today: Coins. Canada's penny was withdrawn around 2013 **[unverified]**. | US/Canada: Canada only, a good comment starter. | Image: A hand at a corner-store counter in Canada holding paper bills, with candy in jars.
