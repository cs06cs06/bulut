# 15 Things Every Family Did on 1960s Road Trips — Unthinkable Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Three kids, one bench seat, zero seat belts. Here are fifteen road trip habits we all took for granted. And number one? Every kid on the block fought for that spot, and today it would get your dad pulled over before he left the driveway.

## #15 Dad Refused to Stop

**s02 · photos**

Dad had one rule on the highway. We stop when we need gas. Not when you need the bathroom. Not when you spot a pony ride. He was making good time, and he was proud of it. So you crossed your legs and watched the telephone poles crawl by. When he finally pulled in, the restroom key hung on a chain, or on a block of wood the size of a cutting board. Today, an app plans every stop for you. Back then, the only plan was Dad's gas gauge. And that needle never seemed to move.

## #14 Free Road Maps and Mom Navigating

**s03 · photos**

Fill up the tank, and the man handed you a road map. Free. Gas stations, car dealers, even hotels gave them away. Mom spread it across her lap like a tablecloth. She traced the red lines with her finger while Dad asked, which turn, which turn? Then came the real test. Folding it back. Nobody ever managed it. It went into the glove box looking like a crumpled accordion. The oil crisis of the early seventies ended most of those free maps. Today, a phone talks you through every turn. But it never once argued with Dad.

## #13 Reading the Rhyming Roadside Signs

**s04 · photos**

Somebody in the back seat would spot the first little red sign. Then the next. Then the next. Each one carried a single line of a silly poem, spaced out along the road. The whole car read them out loud together, waiting for the punch line on the last sign. Those rhyming shaving cream ads ran from nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen sixty-three. Then they came down for good. Only one complete original set survives, at the Smithsonian. Today, at highway speed, you'd never read them. Back then, we slowed down for the joke.

## #12 The License Plate Game

**s05 · photos**

Are we there yet? We asked about every ten minutes. So Mom invented games to keep the peace. The license plate game was the big one. You pressed your nose to the window, hunting for a plate from somewhere far away. Florida. Texas. In the States, a plate from Ontario or Quebec was pure gold. Up in Canada, it was one from California. Then came I Spy, the alphabet game, and counting cows until somebody passed a cemetery and wiped out your whole herd. Today, kids wear headphones. We had a window and each other.

## #11 No Air Conditioning, Hot Vinyl Seats

**s06 · photos**

Plenty of family cars back then had what Dad called two-sixty air conditioning. Two windows down, sixty miles an hour. The wind roared so loud you had to shout. Mom tied a scarf over her hair, and it still came home a bird's nest. That little triangle vent window got aimed straight at your face. And the seats. Dark vinyl, baking in the sun. You wore shorts, you sat down, and you yelped. Then you peeled your legs off like tape. Today, the car is cool before you leave the driveway. We had the wind, and red marks on our legs.

## #10 Thirty Cents a Gallon

**s07 · photos**

Fill 'er up. Dad said it without a second thought. In the early sixties, a gallon of gas averaged about thirty cents in the United States. By nineteen seventy, around thirty-six. The numbers on the pump rolled with a soft clicking sound, and the bell went ding as the next car pulled in. Now here's the surprise. Thirty-one cents in nineteen sixty works out to about three and a half dollars in today's money. So it wasn't quite the bargain we remember. It just felt that way. Dad's paycheck was smaller, and so was the number on the pump.

## #9 The Full-Service Attendant

**s08 · photos**

Ding, ding. You heard that bell hose the second Dad's tires rolled over it. Out came a man in a cap and a pressed uniform. He pumped the gas. He checked the oil and wiped the dipstick on a red rag. He washed the windshield, and often checked the tires too. All while you just sat there. If you were lucky, Dad let you fish a cold bottle of soda out of the chest cooler. Full service was simply normal until about nineteen seventy. Today, we pump our own and pay at the pump. Back then, somebody came running to help you. If that bell just rang in your memory, subscribe, because every week we take another road trip back to the sixties together.

## #8 Roadside Picnics From the Cooler

**s09 · photos**

Restaurants were a treat. Maybe once a trip. The rest of the time, Mom ran a kitchen out of a plaid cooler. Bologna sandwiches in wax paper. Deviled eggs that had been riding in the sun since breakfast. A thermos of coffee for Dad and a jug of sugary fruit drink for us. Dad would find a picnic table under the trees beside the road, and we'd eat with the trucks rumbling by. Nobody worried about the eggs. Today, it's a drive-thru window and a paper bag. Back then, lunch tasted like sunshine and a little road dust.

## #7 Dad Smoking With Windows Up

**s10 · photos**

Dad pushed in the dashboard lighter and waited. Pop. Out it came, glowing orange like a tiny stove. In a lot of cars, the windows stayed up to keep Mom's hair in place, and a gray haze settled over everybody in the back. The ashtray in the dash was full by Kansas. Ashtrays and lighters were just part of the car back then, like the radio. Nobody thought twice. Today, most new cars don't even come with an ashtray, and some places ban smoking in a car with children. Back then, we cracked a window and held our breath.

## #6 Brand-New Highways and Open Roads

**s11 · photos**

Before the big highways, a road trip went straight down every town's Main Street. Stoplights. Detours. Slow trucks. Then in nineteen fifty-six, the interstate law was signed, promising forty-one thousand miles of highway. Year by year, smooth new four-lane roads opened, and Dad grinned like he'd built them himself. In Canada, the Trans-Canada Highway was officially opened at Rogers Pass, British Columbia, in September of nineteen sixty-two. And until nineteen seventy-four, there was no national speed limit in the States. Today, those roads are just the way to work. Back then, a brand-new highway felt like the future.

## #5 Picking Up Hitchhikers

**s12 · photos**

A young man stood on the shoulder with a duffel bag and his thumb out. And in a lot of families, Dad just pulled over. The stranger climbed into the back seat, right next to the kids, and rode along for fifty miles talking about where he was headed. Mom might hand him a sandwich. Nobody thought it was strange. Picking up a hitchhiker was simply being neighborly. Today, almost nobody does it, and most parents would faint at the thought. Back then, a thumb on the highway meant somebody needed a ride, and Dad had room.

## #4 The Neon Motel With a Pool

**s13 · photos**

As the sun went down, the hunt began. Every kid in the car had one job. Find the motel with the pool. Then you saw it. Pink neon buzzing against the evening sky, promising a vacancy, a pool and a television. Dad went into the office to ask about a room, and we held our breath. In nineteen sixty, a room in the hotel business averaged about eleven dollars a night. One California motel chain even named itself after its six-dollar rate. Then came the best part. A night swim in a kidney-shaped pool. Today we book online. Back then, we followed the neon.

## #3 Secret Seats: Shelf, Hump, Front Bench

**s14 · photos**

Every car had secret seats. The littlest one napped on the shelf under the back window, stretched out in the warm sun like a cat. Another kid curled up on the floor hump. And somebody always rode up front, squeezed between Mom and Dad on the bench seat. Babies rode right on Mom's lap. When Dad hit the brakes hard, Mom's arm shot out across your chest. That was the safety equipment. Today, there are car seats and booster seats and rules for every age. Back then, Mom's arm was the seat belt, and we trusted it completely.

## #2 Nobody Buckled Up

**s15 · photos**

Seat belts? Sure, a lot of cars had them. They were somewhere down in the crack of the seat, with the crayons and the pennies. Starting in nineteen sixty-eight, new cars in the States had to come with lap and shoulder belts for the front seats. But wearing them was another story. Metal dashboards. No headrests. Nobody clicked anything. The first state law making people buckle up was New York's, in the mid-eighties. Ontario got there first, in nineteen seventy-six. Today, police in most states can stop you just for skipping it. Back then, the belt was just something you sat on.

## #1 The Way-Back of the Station Wagon

**s16 · photos**

And here it is. The spot every kid on the block fought over. The way-back of the station wagon. Dad folded the seat flat, Mom spread out the sleeping bags, and that was your bedroom on wheels. You played cards. You napped. You waved at the truckers behind you until one blew his horn. Some wagons even had a third seat facing backwards. In the fifties and sixties, about one car in five sold in the States was a station wagon. Today, a child loose in the cargo area would get Dad pulled over before he left the driveway. Back then, it was the best seat in the house.
