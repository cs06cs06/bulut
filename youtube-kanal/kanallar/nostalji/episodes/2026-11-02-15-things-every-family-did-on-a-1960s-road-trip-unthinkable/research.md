# Research: 15 Things Every Family Did on a 1960s Road Trip — Unthinkable Today

> **Scriptwriter note:** My search tools hit their usage limit partway through, so I could only check part of the list against sources. Anything with a URL comes from a page I read. Anything marked **(unverified)** comes from general knowledge, so state it softly ("many families…", "by the late '60s…") or check it before using it. The items with the strongest sources are #1, #2, #5, #6, #7, #12, #16, #17, #18, #20 and #27.

## Candidate items

1. **Nobody buckled up** — Details: the belts were often stuffed down the seat crack or missing entirely; cars had metal dashboards, no headrests, and Dad's arm shot out when he braked hard. | Facts: carmakers started putting seat belts in US passenger cars in the late 1950s. From 1968, federal rules required lap and shoulder belts in the front outboard seats of new cars, with some conditions. Built-in three-point belts became standard in 1973 (cdc.gov). The first US state law requiring people to wear belts was New York's in 1984. It took effect Dec 1984 or 1985 (sources differ), and before it only about 16% of New Yorkers buckled up (trafficsafety.ny.gov; nysenate.gov). By 1990, 37 more states had passed similar laws (Wikipedia: Click It or Ticket). Cars of the '50s and '60s also lacked collapsible steering columns (belairdirect.com). | Today: wearing a belt is the law almost everywhere, and most states let police stop you just for not wearing one. | US/CA: Ontario was the first province to require belts, on Jan 1, 1976, with Quebec next (Wikipedia: Seat belt legislation in Canada). | Image: a dusty bench seat in the sun with an unused belt buckle poking out of the crack, and three kids leaning over the front seat.

2. **Kids in the "way-back" of the station wagon** — Details: sleeping bags spread over the folded cargo floor, kids lying down, playing cards and waving at the truckers behind them; some wagons had a rear-facing third seat. | Facts: in the '50s and '60s, station wagons made up about one in five cars sold in the US (carbuzz.com). Some 1960s wagons offered rear-facing fold-away third seats, and one 1963 model even had a sliding roof over the back (ebay.com/motors blog). Full-size wagon sales dropped sharply in 1974–75, and minivans arrived in 1984 (carbuzz.com; autoblog.com). | Today: no child would ride loose in a cargo area. Families moved to minivans and then SUVs. | US/CA: the same vehicles were sold on both sides of the border. | Image: a 1960s wagon with its tailgate window down, two freckled kids waving out the back on a two-lane highway at golden hour.

3. **The back-window shelf, the floor hump and the front bench** — Details: the smallest child napped on the ledge under the rear window, a sibling curled up on the floor hump, and a third kid rode up front between Mom and Dad. Mom's arm worked as the "seat belt." | Facts: these were common family habits with no specific dates **(unverified)**. | Today: unthinkable, since child seats and belts are now required. | Image: a toddler asleep on the sunny rear window ledge, seen from outside the car.

4. **Babies on Mom's lap, no real car seats** — Details: infants rode in a bassinet or on a lap. Early "car seats" were little hook-on boosters made so the child could see out, not to protect them in a crash. | Facts: the first US federal standard for child seats came around 1971, and Tennessee passed the first state child-restraint law around 1977–78 **(unverified)**. | Today: rear-facing seats are mandatory, and many fire stations will check how they're installed. | Image: a mother in the front seat with a baby in her arms, a road map on the dashboard.

5. **Free paper road maps, and Mom as navigator** — Details: an oil-company map came free with a fill-up. Nobody could fold it back the right way, and arguments over "the next turn" were part of the trip. | Facts: the 1950s to early 1970s were the golden age of the free road map. Car dealers and hotels gave them out too, and the gas crisis of the early 1970s made many oil companies stop printing them (worthpoint.com). Free gas-station maps were basically gone by 1980 (populartimelines.com). | Today: GPS and phones; paper maps now cost money. | Image: a map spread across a station-wagon dashboard, Mom tracing the route with her finger and Dad glancing over.

6. **The full-service attendant** — Details: the bell hose went *ding-ding*, and a man in a cap and uniform pumped your gas, checked the oil, washed the windshield and often checked the tires. Kids could get a soda from a chest cooler. | Facts: full service was the normal US station until about 1970, and self-service was unusual (populartimelines.com). The first US self-serve station opened in Los Angeles in 1947, and Canada's first opened in Winnipeg in 1949. New Jersey banned self-serve in 1949 and Oregon in 1951. Oregon allowed self-serve statewide in 2023 but kept full service as an option (populartimelines.com). Gas was leaded, and unleaded came in for cars with catalytic converters around 1974–75 **(unverified)**. | Today: almost everyone pumps their own gas and pays at the pump. | US/CA: Canada had self-serve early (Winnipeg 1949), but full service was still common there in the '60s **(unverified)**. | Image: an attendant cleaning a windshield while a boy watches from the back seat, with a lit sign in the background.

7. **Gas for about 30 cents a gallon** — Details: "Fill 'er up" with no worrying about the cost, and a price sign with hand-changed numbers. | Facts: the US national average was about 30–31¢ a gallon from 1960 to 1965 and about 36¢ in 1970 (creditdonkey.com; mentalfloss.com). Mental Floss says 31¢ in 1960 equals about $3.51 in 2026 dollars, so the price was cheap mostly in nominal terms. | Today: prices are higher nominally, but in real terms the gap is smaller than people remember. That's a good "surprise" line. | US/CA: Canada sold gas by the imperial gallon, which is bigger than the US gallon, before switching to litres **(unverified)**. Don't compare prices directly. | Image: a close-up of an old pump with spinning number dials.

8. **Gas-station giveaways and trading stamps** — Details: drinking glasses, steak knives, stamps pasted into booklets, and kids competing over who got to lick them. | Facts: gas stations commonly gave out premiums and trading stamps in the '60s and early '70s **(unverified, no dates or prices)**. | Today: loyalty apps. | Image: a kitchen table with stamp booklets and a set of gas-station glasses.

9. **Dad smoking with the windows rolled up** — Details: the push-in lighter popped out glowing orange, the dashboard ashtray overflowed, and a gray haze filled the car. | Facts: ashtrays and lighters were standard equipment in 1960s cars **(unverified)**. | Today: most cars come without ashtrays, and some provinces and states ban smoking in cars with children **(unverified, check which ones)**. | Image: a hand pressing in a chrome dashboard lighter as morning light comes through a smoky windshield.

10. **No air conditioning: windows down, legs stuck to the vinyl** — Details: the small triangular vent window aimed at your face, hair whipping, and hot vinyl seats burning the backs of your legs. The old joke was "2-60 air conditioning": two windows down at 60 mph. | Facts: vent windows were common in 1960s cars and faded out as air conditioning spread **(unverified)**. Use no adoption percentages; I couldn't verify any. | Today: AC is standard. | Image: a girl's arm out the window "flying" her hand in the wind.

11. **The canvas water bag and the overheated radiator** — Details: a damp canvas bag hung on the bumper or grille to keep water cool for the desert, and the car sat by the road with its hood up and steam rising on mountain grades. | Facts: **(unverified)**. | Today: modern cooling systems rarely overheat. | Image: a family waiting on a desert roadside with the hood up and a water bag dripping.

12. **Reading the rhyming roadside signs out loud** — Details: small red signs spaced along the road, a joke in verse with the last line as the punchline, and kids shouting out each line. | Facts: the shaving-cream sign campaign ran from 1927 to 1963. The company was sold in 1963, the signs came down, and the product was gone by 1966 (pa-trolley.org; printmag.com). Rising upkeep costs helped end the signs (printmag.com). Only one complete original set survives, at the Smithsonian (azdot.gov). Keep the brand mention historical and minimal. | Today: these signs don't work at highway speeds. | Image: a line of small red signs along a wheat field and a two-lane road.

13. **Car games: license plates, I Spy, cow counting** — Details: hunting for out-of-state or out-of-province plates, the alphabet game on signs, and "Are we there yet?" every ten minutes. | Facts: common family memories with no firm dates **(unverified)**. | Today: kids have tablets and headphones. | US/CA: spotting a "Canada plate" or a "US plate" was a highlight. | Image: two kids pressed to the window pointing at a passing plate.

14. **AM radio fading between towns** — Details: a station would fade into static, Dad turned the knob, and you heard farm reports and the local news. Only Dad changed the station. | Facts: FM radios were rare in cars, and factory 8-track players arrived around the mid-1960s **(unverified)**. | Today: satellite radio and streaming. | Image: a hand on a chrome radio knob, the dial glowing on the dashboard.

15. **"We'll stop when we need gas": Dad refusing to stop** — Details: he was proud of "making good time," and the bathroom meant a gas station with the key chained to a huge tag. | Facts: rest areas were built along the new interstates **(unverified, no dates)**. | Today: frequent rest areas and stops planned on an app. | Image: a hand holding a gas-station restroom key on an oversized wooden paddle.

16. **The interstate was brand new, or not built yet** — Details: driving through every town's main street, stoplights and detours, then the thrill of a new four-lane road. | Facts: the Federal-Aid Highway Act was signed on June 29, 1956, and authorized 41,000 miles of interstate, the largest public works project in US history at the time (fhwa.dot.gov; history.com). Before that, mapmakers had to update maps every year (worthpoint.com). | Today: a finished interstate network. | Image: a family car passing a "New Highway Opening" sign and a construction crew.

17. **No national speed limit** — Details: you drove "as fast as the car would go" on open western roads. | Facts: the 55 mph National Maximum Speed Law was signed on Jan 2, 1974, to save fuel during the oil crisis (streetsblog.org; Wikipedia). Before then, limits differed by state, and some states already had 50–55 mph limits (wiki.aaroads.com). Road deaths fell 16.4% in the following year, from 54,052 (PMC/NIH). | Today: limits are set by states again (repealed in 1995, **unverified**). | US/CA: Canada sets limits by province, and they were posted in mph until metric conversion. | Image: a car on an empty desert highway with heat shimmer.

18. **The motel neon sign: "VACANCY – POOL – TV"** — Details: hunting for a motel in the evening, the pink neon flickering, the kids begging for "the one with the pool," then a kidney-shaped pool and a nighttime swim. | Facts: average US hotel-industry room rates were about $5.91 in 1950, $10.81 in 1960 and $19.83 in 1970 (choicehotels.com; travelpulse.com). These are industry averages, not motels specifically; roadside motels were often cheaper **(unverified)**. | Today: booking ahead online, chain hotels, fewer outdoor pools. | Image: a family in a station wagon pulling into a small motel lit by neon at dusk.

19. **Motel extras: the ice machine, the key fob and the coin-operated bed** — Details: Dad sent a kid with the ice bucket, the room key hung on a big plastic diamond, and a coin-operated vibrating bed cost a quarter. | Facts: **(unverified)**; avoid the brand name. | Today: key cards. | Image: a child's hand pressing a button on an ice machine in a lit breezeway.

20. **The $6 budget motel** — Details: basic rooms with no frills. | Facts: one budget chain was founded in California and named for its $6 nightly rate (about $64 in 2025 dollars). By 1965 it had opened its 15th motel and first one outside California (Wikipedia: Motel 6). Founding year 1962 **(unverified)**. | Today: the name stayed, the price didn't. That's an optional "fun fact." | Image: a plain motel row with a hand-painted price sign.

21. **Picnic tables, the cooler and the thermos** — Details: sandwiches wrapped in wax paper, a jug of sugary fruit drink, deviled eggs, and a picnic table under the trees by the road. Drive-in restaurants were a once-a-trip treat. | Facts: **(unverified)**. | Today: fast-food drive-thrus. | Image: a family around a roadside picnic table with a plaid cooler and thermos.

22. **Tent camping and pop-up trailers** — Details: canvas tents, a hissing lantern, a campfire, setting up in the dark in the rain. | Facts: national park visits boomed in the '60s **(unverified, no numbers)**. | US/CA: provincial parks in Canada; a national park visit was a classic of the new Trans-Canada era. | Image: a canvas tent glowing from a lantern, a station wagon behind it.

23. **Picking up hitchhikers** — Details: a young man with his thumb out, Dad pulling over and the stranger climbing in back with the kids. | Facts: **(unverified)**. | Today: almost nobody does it, and it's illegal in some places **(unverified)**. | Image: a young hitchhiker with a duffel bag on a two-lane road.

24. **Litter out the window** — Details: candy wrappers and soda bottles thrown from the car without a thought. | Facts: the federal Highway Beautification Act of 1965 targeted billboards and junkyards, and anti-litter campaigns grew in the late '60s and early '70s **(unverified; keep politics out)**. | Today: heavy fines and a strong social taboo. | Image: an anti-litter sign by the road (don't stage actual littering).

25. **Postcards and the vacation slide show** — Details: buying postcards at the souvenir shop, waiting for the film to come back, then watching slides on a screen in the living room with the whole family. Pennants and car stickers were souvenirs too. | Facts: cartridge cameras around 1963 and postcard postage of a few cents **(unverified)**. | Today: instant photos on phones. | Image: a family in a dark living room, a slide projector beam showing a canyon on the screen.

26. **Calling home: the pay phone trick** — Details: a collect or person-to-person call to Grandma: "Asking for myself" meant "we arrived safely," and the call was declined so nobody paid. | Facts: **(unverified)**. | Today: a text. | Image: Dad in a phone booth by the road, the family waiting in the car.

27. **(Canada) The brand-new Trans-Canada Highway** — Details: the final stretch through Rogers Pass opened, and families crossed the mountains. | Facts: the federal government officially opened the Trans-Canada Highway at Rogers Pass in Glacier National Park, BC, on Sept 3, 1962 (parks.canada.ca). A provincial opening event was held first, on July 30, 1962 (tranbc.ca), and historians note the date has been disputed (niche-canada.org). | Today: a busy main route. | US/CA: this was a Canadian milestone; the US counterpart is the interstates (#16). | Image: a car among the mountain peaks on the new highway, with a maple-leaf sign.

28. **(US/CA) Crossing the border with just a driver's license, and miles before kilometres** — Details: one question from the officer ("Where are you from?") and you were through. | Facts: Canadian road signs switched to km/h around 1977, and passports became required at land crossings around 2009 **(unverified)**. | Today: passports and enhanced IDs. | Image: a booth at a small 1960s border crossing with a family car in line.
