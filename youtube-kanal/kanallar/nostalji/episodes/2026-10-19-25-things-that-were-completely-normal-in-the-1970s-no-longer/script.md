# 15 Things We Did in the 1970s That Are No Longer Allowed Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

A metal-tipped lawn dart, falling toward the family picnic. Fifteen things we did without thinking twice, all off-limits today. And number one? We did it at thirty thousand feet, with an ashtray right in the seat, and today, security would be waiting at the gate.

## #15 Riding in the Back of the Pickup

**s02 · photos**

Remember the back of the pickup? Three or four of us kids perched on the wheel wells, hanging on to the side rails. Every bump on that gravel road lifted us an inch off the hot metal. Wind in our hair, dust in our teeth, the dog grinning right along with us. We rode home from the lake like that. From the hayfield. From the dump. Today it's restricted in many states, especially for kids. Funny thing is, about twenty states still have no law about it at all. But back then, nobody even asked. The truck bed was the best seat in the house.

## #14 Hot Steel Slides Over Concrete

**s03 · photos**

In July, the big steel slide at the schoolyard could just about cook an egg. We climbed that tall ladder anyway, sat on our hands, and screamed all the way down. Bare legs met hot metal. You learned fast to wear long shorts, or bring a sheet of wax paper to go even faster. Then there was the merry-go-round, spun by the biggest kid until somebody flew off. And under it all? Concrete. Asphalt. Packed dirt if you were lucky. Today those tall slides are mostly gone, replaced by plastic and soft rubber ground. We just called it recess.

## #13 Burning Leaves at the Curb

**s04 · photos**

Every fall, Saturday smelled the same. Dad raked the maple leaves into a big pile at the curb, struck a match, and that sweet, smoky smell drifted down the whole street. We kids watched from the porch steps, close enough to feel the warmth on our faces. Some families kept a rusty burn barrel out back for the household trash, too. Nobody called it pollution. It was just autumn. Today, in a lot of towns, it's not allowed anymore. Now it's paper bags, compost bins and a truck on Tuesday. Cleaner air, sure. But ask anyone our age what fall smelled like.

## #12 Full Service, Leaded, Fill 'er Up

**s05 · photos**

Ding-ding. That bell hose on the driveway told the attendant you'd arrived. Out he came in his cap and coveralls. He pumped the gas, washed the windshield, checked the oil, and sometimes the tires too. You never left the car. And that sharp gasoline smell? A lot of it was leaded gas back then. Canada took leaded gasoline off the market in December of nineteen ninety, and the US followed some years later. Today it's unleaded only, and in most places you pump it yourself. Back then, a fill-up came with a clean windshield and a smile.

## #11 Lead Paint on the Windowsill

**s06 · photos**

Our houses wore paint like armor. Layer after glossy layer on the kitchen trim, the porch railings, the windowsills, even the crib. When it chipped, it came off in thick little flakes. And toddlers being toddlers, some of those flakes ended up in little mouths. Nobody worried. Paint was paint. In the US, the Consumer Product Safety Commission banned lead paint for home use in nineteen seventy-eight. But the old paint already on the walls stayed right where it was. Today, even scraping an old windowsill comes with rules. Back then, Dad just sanded it on a Saturday, no mask, windows open.

## #10 Chasing the Mosquito Fogging Truck

**s07 · photos**

On summer evenings, you heard it before you saw it. A low rumble coming down the street, and behind it, a thick white cloud. The mosquito truck! Every kid on the block grabbed a bike and pedaled straight into the fog. It smelled sweet and strange. You couldn't see your own handlebars. We came out the other side coughing and laughing, then turned around and did it again. Did anyone ask what was in that cloud? Not on our street. Today, kids are told to stay well away from the spraying. Back then, it was the best parade of the summer.

## #9 Walking Grandma Right to the Gate

**s08 · photos**

Remember meeting Grandma at the airport? Not at the curb. Not at a security line. Right at the gate. No ticket needed. The whole family walked down the concourse together, through a simple metal detector, and sat in those orange vinyl seats by the big windows. We pressed our noses to the glass as her plane taxied in. Then she came up the jet bridge, and we were the very first hugs she got. Goodbyes worked the same way. We waved until the plane pulled away. These days, without a ticket, goodbyes happen at the security line. We had them at the window. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another slow trip back to the way we lived.

## #8 Eighteen and Legal at the Bar

**s09 · photos**

In a lot of states, turning eighteen meant more than a diploma. After the voting age dropped to eighteen in nineteen seventy-one, thirty states lowered their drinking age to eighteen, nineteen or twenty. So that graduation night might end at the local tavern. Wood paneling, a glowing jukebox, and a cold beer that was perfectly legal. College towns were packed on Friday nights. Then a national law took effect in nineteen eighty-four, pushing every state toward twenty-one. Today, it's twenty-one everywhere in the US. Up in Canada, it's still eighteen or nineteen, depending on the province. So for Canadians, this one never really changed.

## #7 Running to the Store for Mom's Cigarettes

**s10 · photos**

Here's one the grandkids won't believe. Mom handed you a few coins and a note in her handwriting. Off you went to the corner store, maybe nine years old, sneakers slapping the sidewalk. You slid the note across the counter. The man read it, nodded, reached behind him, and handed over a pack of cigarettes. Maybe a piece of candy for your trouble. Nobody asked for ID. Nobody called anybody. Today, selling tobacco to a minor is an offense, and clerks card folks who look forty. Back then, a note from Mom was all the ID you needed.

## #6 Ashtrays in Every Room in Town

**s11 · photos**

Close your eyes and you can still smell it. Smoke drifting from the next restaurant table. A cut-glass ashtray on the bank counter. A haze rolling out of the teachers' lounge every time the door swung open. Offices, buses, hospital waiting rooms, grocery stores. If there was a flat surface, there was an ashtray on it. In Canada, Ottawa passed the country's first city bylaw limiting smoking in indoor public spaces in nineteen seventy-six. Vancouver went further in nineteen ninety-six, with fully smoke-free restaurants. In the US, it happened city by city, state by state. Today, the air is clear. The ashtrays? Antiques.

## #5 No Helmets, Home by Streetlights

**s12 · photos**

After breakfast, we were gone. Banana seat, high handlebars, a baseball card clothespinned to the spokes so it sounded like a motorcycle. No helmet. Nobody even owned one. We rode to the creek, the ball field, the other side of town. Mom didn't know where we were, and she didn't need to. The rule was simple. Be home when the streetlights come on. Today, many places require helmets, especially for kids, and some Canadian provinces require them for everyone. And letting a nine-year-old roam all day? Unthinkable. Back then, the whole neighborhood was our backyard.

## #4 Baby on Mom's Lap, Kids in Back

**s13 · photos**

Car seat? We had Mom. The baby rode up front, right on her lap, while Dad drove. The rest of us sprawled in the way back of the station wagon, on sleeping bags, between the cooler and the suitcases. The littlest one sometimes curled up on the rear window shelf to nap in the sun. Tennessee passed the first child restraint law, starting in nineteen seventy-eight, for kids under four. It even had a babes in arms exception that still let a parent hold a child. By nineteen eighty-five, all fifty states had a law. Back then, the station wagon was a rolling living room.

## #3 Seatbelts Buried in the Seat

**s14 · photos**

The seatbelts were there. Somewhere. Usually buried deep in the crack of the bench seat, along with a few pennies and last summer's crayons. Kids stood up on the front seat. Kids knelt backward and waved at the car behind us. And when Dad hit the brakes hard, out shot Mom's right arm, straight across your chest. That was our seatbelt. Ontario was the first to require buckling up, on January first, nineteen seventy-six. In the US, New York was first, in nineteen eighty-four. Today, buckling up is the law almost everywhere. Mom's arm finally got to retire.

## #2 Lawn Darts at the Family Picnic

**s15 · photos**

Picture the family picnic. Burgers on the grill. Uncles in lawn chairs. And in the middle of it all, a game of heavy darts with weighted metal tips. You tossed them underhand, high in the air, aiming for a plastic ring on the grass. Thunk. Straight into the lawn. Dad hollered for everybody to stand back, and the kids took maybe one step. How nobody got hurt at our house, I'll never know. In Canada alone, there were at least fifty-five serious injuries. The US banned their sale in nineteen eighty-eight. Canada followed in July of nineteen eighty-nine. Today, they only come in plastic.

## #1 Smoking at Thirty Thousand Feet

**s16 · photos**

And number one. Remember flying in the seventies? Wide seats. A stewardess with a coffee pot. Men in sport coats, ladies in their best dresses. And right there in your armrest, a little metal ashtray that flipped open with a click. You booked smoking or non-smoking, but it was all one cabin. A blue haze hung under the reading lights. The no smoking sign only lit up for takeoff and landing. Canada banned smoking on all domestic flights in nineteen eighty-nine. The US started with short flights in nineteen eighty-eight, and by two thousand it was every flight. Today, light up up there, and security will be waiting at the gate.
