# Research: 15 Things That Were Completely Normal in the 1970s — No Longer Allowed Today

**Key:** ✅ = confirmed by a source this session · **[unverified]** = from memory only; check it before stating it as fact

## Candidate items

1. **Smoking on airplanes** — Details: an ashtray in every armrest; you picked a "smoking" or "non-smoking" row, but it was all one cabin; a blue haze under the reading lights; the "No Smoking" sign lit up only for takeoff and landing. | Facts ✅: US banned smoking on domestic flights under 2 hours in 1988, under 6 hours in 1990, and on all domestic and international flights to and from the US in 2000. Sources disagree on the exact 1988 start date (Jan 1 vs. late April), so just say "1988" (csts.ua.edu/cartoons/government/airlines/; simpleflying.com/world-timeline-smoking-ban-planes/). Canada banned smoking on all domestic flights in 1989 (globalnews.ca). In Sept 1988 the country's largest airline became the first Canadian airline to go fully smoke-free (aircanada.com facts page). By 1994 Canada banned smoking on international flights leaving Canada (capstone.capilanou.ca). | Today: no smoking at all; the ashtray is gone, though planes still have a "no smoking" sign. | US/Canada: Canada banned it on all domestic flights about 10 years before the US did. | Image: a 1970s cabin with wide seats, a man in a wide-collar shirt tapping ash into the armrest tray, smoke curling in window light, and a stewardess with a coffee pot in the aisle.

2. **Kids riding in the back of the pickup** — Details: wind in your hair, sitting on the wheel wells, grabbing the side rails over bumps, riding home from the lake, the hayfield or the dump. | Facts ✅: even today, Alaska, Delaware, Idaho and 17 other states (about 20 total) have no law about passengers in truck beds. Others allow it only for adults or with exceptions, such as farm work or parades (findlaw.com legal blog; slashgear.com/1733264). | Today: widely restricted, especially for minors, and seen as very dangerous. Do NOT say "illegal everywhere." Better: "now restricted in many states." | US/Canada: Canadian provinces generally require every passenger to be in a seat with a belt, which effectively rules out riding in the box **[unverified]**. | Image: a dusty country road at golden hour, three kids in striped T-shirts and a dog in an old pickup bed, a farmhouse behind them.

3. **No seatbelts, or belts tucked into the seat crack** — Details: belts buried under the bench seat; a kid standing on the front seat; Mom's arm shooting out at a hard stop; a hot vinyl seat. | Facts ✅: New York passed the first US law requiring seatbelt use in 1984 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_belt_legislation; ebsco.com). Ontario was the first Canadian province, effective Jan 1, 1976. Nova Scotia followed Jan 1, 1985 and PEI July 1, 1987 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_belt_legislation_in_Canada). Belts had to be installed in new US cars from the late 1960s **[unverified: 1968]**. New Hampshire still has no adult belt-use law **[unverified]**. | Today: buckling up is required almost everywhere, with "Click It or Ticket" campaigns. | US/Canada: Canada required belt use about 8 years before any US state. | Image: the inside of a big 1970s sedan with a bench seat, belts hanging unused, a kid kneeling backwards on the front seat waving out the rear window.

4. **No child car seats; babies on Mom's lap** — Details: the baby held in the front seat; kids rolling around in the "way back" of the station wagon; sleeping on the rear window shelf. | Facts ✅: Tennessee's child restraint law took effect Jan 1, 1978, for children under 4. It had a "Babes in Arms" exception that still allowed a child to be held in an adult's lap. All 50 states had child restraint laws by 1985 (tntrafficsafety.org/cps; tennesseehistory.org; pubmed 434275). | Today: rear-facing seats, booster seats, and rules by age, weight and height. | US/Canada: Canadian provinces passed their own rules on a different timeline **[unverified dates]**. | Image: a 1970s station wagon on a highway shoulder with the tailgate open, kids lying on sleeping bags in the cargo area, a cooler and a road map.

5. **Lawn darts at the family picnic** — Details: heavy darts with weighted metal tips thrown underhand at a plastic ring; the thunk as they stuck in the grass; Dad yelling "stand back!" | Facts ✅: the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) banned their sale in 1988. Canada banned sales in July 1989, after at least 55 serious injuries there (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawn_darts; thehustle.co). Don't use the claim that owning them was banned; one source says so, but it's unreliable. | Today: only plastic or soft-tip versions are sold. | US/Canada: banned in both, Canada one year later. | Image: a backyard barbecue, a dart in mid-air over green grass, kids in shorts too close to the ring, adults in lawn chairs.

6. **Smoking almost everywhere indoors** — Details: ashtrays in offices, banks, grocery stores, hospital waiting rooms, buses and teachers' lounges; smoke at restaurant tables; the doctor smoking during an appointment **[unverified as common]**. | Facts ✅: Ottawa passed Canada's first city bylaw limiting smoking in indoor public spaces in 1976 (globalnews.ca). Federal office buildings across Canada went smoke-free, outside designated areas, by 1989. Vancouver was the first city to require fully smoke-free restaurants, in 1996 (globalnews.ca; capstone.capilanou.ca). | Today: indoor public smoking is banned in most provinces and states. | US/Canada: in the US the rules come from states and cities, so there was no single national date. | Image: a 1970s office with typewriters, a cut-glass ashtray full of butts, a man in a tie smoking and filing papers, sunlight through the blinds.

7. **Cigarette commercials on TV** — Details: jingles, cowboys and catchy slogans during prime time and sports broadcasts. | Facts ✅: radio and TV cigarette ads ended in the US on Jan 2, 1971 (ebsco.com "Cigarette advertising ban"; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_in_radio). | Today: no tobacco ads on broadcast media. | US/Canada: Canadian broadcasters stopped voluntarily around 1972 **[unverified]**. The first voluntary warning on Canadian cigarette packs came in 1972, and mandatory warnings in 1989 ✅ (globalnews.ca). | Image: a family on a plaid couch in front of a wood-cabinet TV, Dad holding a cigarette, the screen showing a blurry ad.

8. **Sending kids to the corner store to buy cigarettes for Mom or Dad** — Details: a handwritten note, coins in your pocket, the shopkeeper handing the pack over the counter without a second look. | Facts **[unverified]**: the US tied age-of-sale enforcement to federal funding through the Synar Amendment (1992). Canada passed the Tobacco Sales to Young Persons Act around 1993–94. | Today: ID checks; selling to a minor is an offense. | US/Canada: both changed in the early 1990s **[unverified]**. | Image: a small corner store with a candy rack, a boy about 9 years old in a striped T-shirt holding a note up to the clerk, cigarette packs behind the counter.

9. **A drinking age of 18 in many states** — Details: celebrating a high-school graduation with a legal beer; college towns full of young bar crowds. | Facts ✅: after the 26th Amendment lowered the voting age to 18 in July 1971, 30 states lowered their drinking age to 18, 19 or 20. By 1982 only 14 states were still at 21. The National Minimum Drinking Age Act took effect July 17, 1984. South Dakota and Wyoming were the last states to switch (britannica.com/procon/drinking-age-debate; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Minimum_Drinking_Age_Act). | Today: 21 in every state. | US/Canada: big difference. Canada's drinking age is still 18 or 19, depending on the province, so this item is mostly a US story. | Image: a small-town bar with wood paneling, young people in bell-bottoms toasting, a jukebox glowing.

10. **Lead paint on walls, cribs and windowsills** — Details: thick glossy layers; paint chipping on porch railings; kids chewing on windowsills. | Facts ✅: the CPSC banned lead paint for home use in 1978, setting a 0.06% limit. Old paint already on walls stayed (truetips.blog; inspectandtest.net; ebsco.com). | Today: rules for lead-safe renovation. | US/Canada: Canada's limits were set separately **[unverified dates]**. | Image: a sunlit 1950s kitchen with chipped cream-colored paint on a windowsill and a toddler's hand near it.

11. **Leaded gas at the full-service pump** — Details: an attendant pumping your gas, washing the windshield and checking the oil; the gas smell; the "ding-ding" bell hose on the driveway. | Facts ✅: Canada took leaded gasoline off the market in December 1990 (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov PMC3222495; autotrader.ca). The US banned it for on-road vehicles in 1996 **[1996 not directly confirmed; well known]**. | Today: only unleaded; mostly self-serve. | US/Canada: Canada about 5 years earlier. | Image: a 1970s gas station with round-top pumps, an attendant in a cap washing a windshield, the price sign in cents per gallon or per litre.

12. **Flammable children's pajamas** — Details: soft cotton flannel nightgowns, often worn near space heaters and fireplaces. | Facts **[unverified]**: US flammability standards for children's sleepwear came in the early 1970s: sizes 0–6X around 1971–72 and sizes 7–14 around 1974–75. Tris, a flame-retardant chemical, was later pulled from pajamas around 1977. | Today: sleepwear must be flame-resistant or snug-fitting. | US/Canada: Canada had its own rules **[unverified]**. | Image: kids in flannel pajamas in front of a fireplace on Christmas morning.

13. **Three-wheeler ATVs for kids** — Details: balloon tires, a growling engine, kids riding in fields and on dirt trails without helmets. | Facts **[unverified]**: in 1988 an agreement with US federal regulators (a consent decree) ended sales of new three-wheelers. | Today: four-wheelers, with age guidance. | US/Canada: Canada also stopped selling them **[unverified]**. | Image: a farm field in the late 1970s, a teen on a red three-wheeler kicking up dust, no helmet.

14. **Walking Grandma right to the airplane gate** — Details: no ticket needed; meeting family as they walked off the jet bridge; quick security with a simple metal detector. | Facts **[unverified, but widely known]**: after Sept 2001, only ticketed passengers could go past airport security. | Today: goodbyes happen at the security line. | US/Canada: the same in both. | Image: an airport gate in the 1970s, a family waving through big windows, a jet outside, orange vinyl seats.

15. **Hot metal slides and playgrounds over asphalt** — Details: tall steel slides that burned your legs in July; spinning merry-go-rounds; teeter-totters (seesaws) and concrete under the swings. | Facts **[unverified]**: the CPSC published its first public playground safety handbook around 1981. Safer surfaces like wood chips and rubber became the norm in the 1980s and 1990s. These are mostly guidelines and liability practice, not a ban. Phrase it as "mostly gone." | Today: plastic slides, low platforms, soft surfaces. | US/Canada: Canada uses its own playground standard **[unverified dates]**. | Image: a schoolyard with a 12-foot slide shining in the sun, kids in sneakers lining up, a merry-go-round blurred with motion.

16. **Riding bikes without helmets** — Details: banana seats, baseball cards clipped to the spokes, gone until the streetlights came on. | Facts **[unverified]**: there were no helmet laws in the 1970s. Ontario required helmets for under-18s around 1995, and BC required them for all ages around 1996. Many US states require helmets for minors. | Today: helmets are standard and required in many places. | US/Canada: some Canadian provinces require them for all ages; US rules are mostly for minors and vary by state or city. | Image: a quiet suburban street at dusk, four kids on bikes with high handlebars, no helmets, a dog running alongside.

17. **Hitchhiking** — Details: a thumb out on the highway shoulder, a cardboard sign, rides from strangers. | Facts **[unverified]**: many states restrict hitchhiking on highways and interstates; it is not banned everywhere. | Today: rare and widely seen as unsafe. Avoid the word "illegal." | US/Canada: in Canada it is mostly limited on major highways by provincial rules **[unverified]**. | Image: a two-lane highway in the 1970s, a young man with a backpack and a sign, a station wagon slowing down.

18. **Running behind the mosquito fogging truck** — Details: a white cloud and a sweet chemical smell; kids biking through the fog on summer evenings. | Facts **[unverified]**: the US banned most uses of DDT in 1972; Canada restricted it during the 1970s. Fogging trucks often used other chemicals, so don't claim the fog was DDT. | Today: kids are told to stay away from spraying. | US/Canada: different timing for each chemical. | Image: a suburban street at dusk, a truck trailing a white fog, kids on bikes riding into the mist.

19. **Burning leaves and household trash in the backyard** — Details: a smoky fall smell; a rusty burn barrel; Dad raking leaves into a pile at the curb and lighting them. | Facts **[unverified]**: many cities banned burning in the 1970s, after the 1970 US Clean Air Act and local air-quality rules. | Today: leaf pickup, composting and curbside bins. | US/Canada: decided city by city. | Image: an autumn yard, a man in a plaid coat leaning on a rake beside a smoking pile of leaves, kids watching.

20. **Cribs with wide slats and drop sides** — Details: a painted wooden crib, a bumper pad and toys inside. | Facts **[unverified]**: the first US crib standards came around 1973 and limited slat spacing. Drop-side cribs were banned in the US in 2011 and in Canada around 2016. | Today: fixed-side cribs, with bare sleep spaces recommended. | US/Canada: different years. | Image: a 1970s nursery with a yellow painted crib and a teddy bear.

21. **Ashtrays and cigarette lighters built into every car** — Details: pressing in the lighter and waiting for it to pop out glowing orange; the pull-out ashtray in the dashboard. | Facts **[unverified]**: by the 2000s most new cars had dropped the ashtray, replacing the lighter with a power socket. This was a market change, not a law; say "disappeared," not "banned." | Image: a close-up of a 1970s dashboard, the lighter glowing, the ashtray half open.

22. **Paddling in schools** — Details: the principal's paddle hanging on the office wall. | Facts **[unverified]**: Canada's Supreme Court ruled out physical punishment in schools in 2004. Many US states have since banned it, but not all. | Today: it has mostly ended. Handle this carefully; it is an emotional topic and may come up in comments. | Image: a school hallway in the 1970s, an empty office with a wooden paddle on the wall. Use only the object, no people.

23. **Clackers** — Details: two hard balls on a string swung up and down so they smacked together loudly; heard all over the school playground around 1970–71. | Facts **[unverified]**: some versions shattered, and the toy was restricted or recalled around 1971. Check the details for each country. | Image: a schoolyard, a kid swinging two bright balls on a string, other kids watching.

24. **Chemistry sets with real chemicals** — Details: small glass bottles, an alcohol burner, a stain on the basement workbench. | Facts **[unverified]**: later kits were greatly simplified because of safety and liability concerns. This was a market change, not a ban. | Image: a boy in safety goggles in a basement, a test tube over a small flame, a metal kit box open.

25. **Kids on their own all day** — Details: "Be home for supper"; kids walking to school alone at age 6; waiting in the car while Mom shopped. | Facts **[unverified]**: some states now have laws about leaving children unattended in cars; free-range play is shaped mostly by custom, not law. Present it as "unthinkable today" rather than "illegal." | Image: three kids walking home along a quiet sidewalk with lunch boxes, autumn leaves, no adults around.
