# 15 Christmas Memories From the 1960s Only People Over 60 Remember

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Our Christmas tree was aluminum, and nobody blinked. Fifteen memories like that. And number one? Mom hung it strand by strand and saved every bit each January. In nineteen seventy-two, the government quietly got the makers to stop. You've touched it a hundred times.

## #15 Santa on Military Radar

**s02 · photos**

Christmas Eve. The radio on in the kitchen. And then the announcer said it. The military had Santa on radar. Remember how your heart jumped? It all started by accident. In nineteen fifty-five, a department store in Colorado Springs ran a newspaper ad inviting kids to phone Santa. The number was misprinted. It rang at an air defense command center instead. The officers played along, put Santa on their tracking board, and told the press. They have tracked his sleigh every year since. NORAD belongs to both countries, so Canadian kids listened too. Today it's an app. Back then, a wrong number gave us Santa on radar.

## #14 The Four-Cent Christmas Stamp

**s03 · photos**

Remember the kitchen table in early December? A stack of envelopes, the address book, and Mom licking stamps until her tongue gave out. In nineteen sixty-two, the US Post Office issued its very first Christmas stamp. Four cents. A wreath and two candles. They ordered five hundred million and still ran out, printing nearly eight hundred sixty-two million by year's end. Canada had issued a Christmas stamp back in eighteen ninety-eight, then waited until nineteen sixty-four for another. And the cards that came back? Taped around the door frame, strung across the living room on yarn. Four cents carried a whole year of news.

## #13 Angel Hair That Made Us Itch

**s04 · photos**

It looked like a cloud. It felt like a hundred tiny needles. Angel hair was spun glass, sold in little boxes, and we stretched it over the tree or under the little village on the mantel to make snow. The box said flameproof. It did not say itchy, but every kid found out. Smart parents put on work gloves first. Not-so-smart kids scratched their arms until New Year's. Today we use soft polyester snow, and nobody's fingers sting. But nothing ever glowed quite like angel hair with a light behind it. Beautiful, and it bit back.

## #12 Big Hot Bulbs and Tangled Strings

**s05 · photos**

Every year, the same scene. Dad on the living room floor, surrounded by a snake pit of tangled light strings. Those big bulbs were the size of your thumb, painted red, green, blue and orange, and the paint flaked right off the glass. They got hot. Really hot. Touch one and you learned fast. Dad tested every string and swapped tired bulbs one by one from a shoebox of spares. Mom kept a close eye on the tree water, too. Today's little lights stay cool all season long. Ours could warm your hands.

## #11 Glass Ornaments Thin as Bubbles

**s06 · photos**

They came twelve to a box, nested in little cardboard squares. Glass balls so thin you could almost see through the color. Hold one up to the window and your face came back stretched and silly. We hung them with skinny wire hooks that pricked your fingers. And every year, one slipped. That tiny pop, then a hundred silver splinters across the floor, and Mom saying, don't move, I'll get the broom. Today most ornaments just bounce. Ours shattered, and somehow we loved them even more.

## #10 An Orange in the Toe

**s07 · photos**

Reach all the way down into the toe of your stocking, and there it was. A real orange. Cold, round, and smelling like summer. Next to it, walnuts and filberts in the shell, a candy cane, and if you were lucky, ribbon candy stuck together in its dish. Many of our parents remembered leaner times, when an orange at Christmas felt like a treasure, and they kept the habit for us. Peeling it was a whole event. The spray of oil, the smell on your fingers all morning. Kids today find gift cards down there. We found a little piece of sunshine.

## #9 TV Specials Only Once a Year

**s08 · photos**

You got one shot. The special aired one night, once a year, and if you missed it, you waited a whole year. So we planned. Mom circled the night in the TV listings. Baths early, pajamas on, popcorn in the big bowl. Dad fiddled with the rabbit ears until the picture stopped rolling. Then we sat way too close to the screen, and nobody said a word. A Charlie Brown Christmas first aired in nineteen sixty-five, and the stop-motion reindeer had us all believing. No rewind, no replay. Today everything is on demand. Back then, the whole family showed up on time. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the sixties together.

## #8 Christmas Records From the Tire Store

**s09 · photos**

Here's one the grandkids won't believe. You took the car in for new tires, and you came home with Christmas music. In the sixties and seventies, tire stores sold a new Christmas album every year, usually for a dollar, to folks waiting at the counter. Each year had a new volume number, and lots of families collected them. That record went straight onto the console stereo and stayed there all December. The needle crackled, the room smelled like pine, and Dad pretended he'd only gone in for tires. Today those albums turn up in thrift stores. Snow tires and sleigh bells, same receipt.

## #7 Eaton's, the Parade and Toyland

**s10 · photos**

Canadian friends, this one's yours. North of the border, the big Christmas book often came from Eaton's, which had printed a Christmas catalogue since eighteen ninety-seven. But the real magic was downtown. Eaton's started the Santa Claus Parade back in nineteen oh five, and other Canadian cities copied the idea. We stood on the curb in snow boots, cheeks burning, waiting for that float. Then came Toyland, where Eaton's had hosted Santa since nineteen oh three. Trains circling, fake snow in the windows, Mom's mitten squeezing yours. Eaton's is gone now. But that Toyland still runs in our memories.

## #6 Batteries Not Included

**s11 · photos**

Christmas Eve, long after midnight. Dad on the living room floor with a screwdriver, forty little parts, and instructions that seemed written for somebody else. Mom in her robe, holding the flashlight and biting her lip so she wouldn't laugh. Somehow it all came together by morning. Then came the other problem. The toy needed batteries, and nobody had bought any. In a lot of towns, every store was closed on Christmas Day. So the shiny new robot just sat there, silent. Today there's a store open somewhere every hour. Back then, batteries not included was a tragedy.

## #5 Bubble Lights We Waited For

**s12 · photos**

Then there were the lights that actually did something. Bubble lights. Little glass tubes of colored liquid sitting on the branches like candles. You plugged them in and waited. And waited. Then, one by one, they started to bubble. Kids sat right under the tree calling out, that one's going, that one's going! An accountant came up with the idea in nineteen thirty-eight, and after the war they took off. In December of nineteen forty-six alone, nearly a million were sold. Plenty of us grew up with the set our parents bought. Today they're a collector's treat. Back then, they were the best show in the house.

## #4 The Sears Wish Book

**s13 · photos**

It landed in the mailbox in late summer, thick and heavy. And the house went quiet. The Sears Christmas catalog had been around since nineteen thirty-three, and in nineteen sixty-eight it officially became the Wish Book. We earned that name. We lay on our stomachs on the rug, circling toys in pen, folding down corners, writing our names beside the good stuff so there was no confusion. Brothers fought over who got it first. By December, the toy pages were soft as cloth. In Canada, many families had the Simpsons-Sears book instead. Today a wish list is a link. Ours had fingerprints.

## #3 The Silver Aluminum Tree

**s14 · photos**

And then, one year, the tree came out of a box. Silver aluminum. Shiny. Space-age. Each branch slid out of its own paper sleeve, and you pushed it into a hole in the pole, one by one. The needles crinkled like candy wrappers. No sap, no shedding, and the cat lost interest. Most were made in Manitowoc, Wisconsin, starting in nineteen fifty-nine, for around twenty-five dollars. The makers first built only a few hundred, unsure anyone would buy. Several million ended up in American homes. Then Charlie Brown poked fun at them in nineteen sixty-five. Today a rare pink one sold for over three thousand dollars. Mom kept every sleeve.

## #2 The Turning Color Wheel

**s15 · photos**

But you couldn't string electric lights on a metal tree. That was a fire and shock hazard. So the answer sat on the floor. The color wheel. A little lamp with a motor, turning a disc of red, blue, green and amber. Lights out, and the whole silver tree slowly changed color. Blue. Then red. Then green. You could hear the soft hum of the motor and smell the warm bulb. We lay on the carpet, chins in our hands, and watched it like television. Some didn't even have a motor. They turned from the heat of their own bulb. Back then, it was free magic every night.

## #1 Tinsel, One Strand at a Time

**s16 · photos**

Mom had one rule about tinsel. One strand at a time. Never thrown in clumps, no matter how much we begged. And it hung beautifully, heavy and straight, like real icicles catching the light. In January, she picked off every strand and draped it back over its cardboard for next year. Here's the part nobody told us. For decades, most tinsel sold in the US was made of lead foil. That's why it was so heavy. In nineteen seventy-two, the Food and Drug Administration reached an agreement with the makers to stop. Today's tinsel is plastic, light, and full of static. Ours was lead, and we saved every strand.
