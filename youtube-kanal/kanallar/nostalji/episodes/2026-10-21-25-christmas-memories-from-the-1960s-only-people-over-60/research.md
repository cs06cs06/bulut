# Research: 15 Christmas Memories From the 1960s Only People Over 60 Remember

> **Note on sourcing:** I hit the tool usage limit partway through, so some leads could not be checked. Anything tagged **(unverified)** comes from my background knowledge only, and the script should not state it as fact. Where an item rests on a single collector or commercial page, I say so. Facts with a URL next to them come from the pages I actually searched or read.

## Candidate items

1. **The aluminum Christmas tree** — details: silver "needles" made of foil that crinkled when you fluffed them. Each branch was pulled out of its own paper sleeve and pushed into holes in a wooden-look pole. In the living room it gave off a cool metallic shine and was never bothered by pets or dropped needles. | facts: A Chicago company made the first one in 1958. Most were made by the Aluminum Specialty Co. of Manitowoc, Wisconsin, which brought its version out in 1959 (etsy.com/blog/aluminum-christmas-tree). The company showed it at the American Toy Fair in New York in March 1959 and gave it the "Evergleam" name in 1960. "Several million" ended up in American homes, and production stopped in 1971–72 as sales fell (historicalmuseum.wisconsinhistory.org/explore/ever-gleaming/). It sold for around $25. The company first made only a few hundred because it wasn't sure they would sell. Owners kept the paper sleeves to store the branches (atomic-ranch.com). | today: The fad faded in the late 1960s. The 1965 TV special *A Charlie Brown Christmas* made fun of metal trees, and that is widely said to have hurt them (Wisconsin Hist. Museum; Atomic Ranch). Today an original sells for hundreds to thousands of dollars, and one rare pink tree went for over $3,000 (Atomic Ranch). | US/Canada: Sold in Canada too (vintageinn.ca covers them for Canadian readers). There are no Canadian sales figures **(unverified)**. | image: a 1960s living room at dusk, a 6-ft silver tree on a low table, a child sliding a branch out of its paper sleeve, and the box lying open on a shag rug.

2. **The rotating color wheel** — details: a small floor lamp with a motor that turned a disc of colored panels (red, blue, green, amber). The tree slowly changed color all evening. You could hear a faint motor hum and smell the hot bulb. | facts: People used color wheels because stringing electric lights on a metal tree was a fire and shock hazard (Etsy blog). The Evergleam motorized wheel had a bulb plus a small motor and sat on the floor pointing up at the tree. The company also sold a "Revolving 4-Color Projector" with no motor that turned from the heat of its own bulb, along with revolving tree stands (Wisconsin Hist. Museum). | today: Modern reproductions cost around $35; restored originals run around $200 (Atomic Ranch). | US/Canada: no known difference. | image: a dark room, a pool of turning blue-to-red light on a silver tree, and kids lying on the carpet with their chins in their hands, watching.

3. **The Sears Christmas catalog / "Wish Book"** — details: it came in late summer. Kids wore out the toy pages, circled things in pen, folded down corners and wrote their names next to what they wanted. | facts: The first Sears Christmas Book came out in 1933 (blog.newspapers.com). It reportedly had 87 pages (fooddrinklife.com, quoting Sears Archives; secondary source). It was mailed each year in August or September (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears_Wish_Book). Sears made "Wish Book" the official name in 1968, when big toys included little die-cast cars, action figures and a space-themed astronaut figure. Sears announced in 1993 that it was dropping the book (blog.newspapers.com). | today: Online shopping and wish lists replaced it. The printed catalog as a family ritual has largely gone. | US/Canada: Canada had its own Simpsons-Sears catalog, and Eaton's had a separate Christmas book (see #4). Simpsons-Sears catalog dates are **(unverified)**. | image: two kids on their stomachs on a braided rug with a thick catalog open to the toy pages, one holding a pencil, and a mug of cocoa nearby.

4. **The Eaton's Christmas catalogue & Toyland (Canada)** — details: a Canadian family's version of the Wish Book. The trip downtown to the department store's Toyland was the high point of the season. | facts: Eaton's put out its first Christmas catalogue in 1897, and by 1903 Santa was appearing in the store's toy department (heritagetrust.on.ca). Department stores across Canada each ran their own Toyland. Eaton's mailed children "letters from Santa" inviting them to Toyland and printed mechanical Christmas cards to advertise it (Canadian Museum of History page, museedelaguerre.ca). The Eaton's catalogue ended in 1976 **(unverified)**. | today: Eaton's is gone. The downtown Toyland trip has given way to malls and online shopping. | US/Canada: **This is the main Canadian counterpart to the Sears book.** | image: a mother in a wool coat and hat holding a boy's mittened hand at a department store's Toyland entrance, with model trains and snow-dusted window displays behind them.

5. **Bubble lights** — details: glass tubes of colored liquid that started bubbling a few minutes after you plugged them in. Kids sat and waited for each one to "go." | facts: An accountant invented the bubbling light in 1938. The NOMA company bought the rights but couldn't sell them in quantity until 1946, after the war (oldchristmastreelights.com). In December 1946 NOMA sold nearly one million, and most US homes had some bubble lights for several Christmases after (goldenglow.org). | today: They became a nostalgia item. Reproductions are sold, though fewer families use them. That they stayed common all through the 1960s is **(unverified)**; they started in the late 1940s and carried on into the 60s. | US/Canada: NOMA also had Canadian operations **(unverified)**. | image: a close-up of three bubble lights on a real fir branch, warm glow, a child's face blurred behind them.

6. **Tinsel hung one strand at a time** — details: Mom insisted it go on one strand at a time, never thrown in clumps. After Christmas it was picked off and draped back over its cardboard to use again. It was heavy and hung straight down like icicles. | facts: From roughly the 1930s to the early 1970s, US tinsel was made mostly of lead foil (morbidhistorypod.substack.com). In 1972 the FDA reached an agreement with manufacturers to stop making lead tinsel. Today's tinsel is PVC (mentalfloss.com). Some other versions were reusable year after year, and the aluminum-paper kind was flammable (mentalfloss.com). | today: Lead is restricted in consumer products. Modern plastic tinsel is light and full of static, and many families skip it. | US/Canada: Canadian rules on lead tinsel **(unverified)**. | image: grandma's hand passing single silver strands to a small girl reaching up to a low branch, with a flattened tinsel card on the coffee table.

7. **"Angel hair" spun glass** — details: a cloud of white or pastel glass fiber stretched over the tree or under the nativity set or village to look like snow. It made your fingers itch, and wise parents wore gloves. | facts: Vintage boxes say "Angel Hair," "Flameproof," "Net Wt. 2 oz." (etsy.com market listings). Collector listings describe US-made boxed angel hair from the 1950s (pinterest listing; collector source only). That it irritated skin is common knowledge, and no safety ruling was found **(unverified)**. | today: Replaced by polyester "snow" batting. | US/Canada: none known. | image: a father in a cardigan wearing work gloves, gently pulling a wisp of angel hair over a tabletop village of cardboard houses.

8. **Big, hot screw-in bulbs and the yearly light-string untangling** — details: thumb-sized painted bulbs that got hot to the touch. Paint flaked off the glass. Every year Dad tested strings on the floor and swapped bulbs one by one. | facts: **(unverified)**: In the 1960s most homes used large C7 (indoor) and C9 (outdoor) bulbs. Small "midget" or miniature lights spread in the late 60s–70s, and LEDs much later. | today: LED strings stay cool and use far less power. | US/Canada: none known. | image: a father kneeling on a hardwood floor surrounded by tangled light strings and a shoebox of spare bulbs, with one bulb glowing in his hand.

9. **Thin glass ball ornaments and wire hooks** — details: thin, mirror-bright glass balls in boxes of a dozen, with cardboard dividers. When one shattered it went into a hundred silver pieces. Some faded with a few hand-painted stripes. | facts: **(unverified)**: US machine-made glass ornaments were mass-produced from the late 1930s and were very common in 1950s–60s homes. Plastic and shatterproof ornaments later took over. | today: Mostly plastic or shatterproof. Originals are collected. | US/Canada: none known. | image: an open ornament box on the floor, a child holding a glass ball up to the window light and seeing a bent reflection of their face.

10. **Christmas albums from the tire store** — details: you picked up the year's Christmas record at the counter while the car was being serviced, and the whole family played it on the console stereo all December. | facts: In the 1960s and '70s, tire dealers sold yearly Christmas albums in their stores, "usually for a dollar each," to customers waiting on tires or alignments (wqxr.org). Volume numbers on collector listings (e.g., Vol. 4 in 1965, Vol. 6 in 1968) show the series came out every year (eBay listings; collector source). | today: Streaming playlists. Store-promotion LPs are now thrift-store finds. *Avoid naming the brands; say "tire stores and service stations."* | US/Canada: whether these were sold in Canada **(unverified)**. | image: a wood console stereo with its lid up, a record spinning, and a teenager reading the back of the record sleeve beside a decorated tree.

11. **TV Christmas specials you could see only once a year** — details: the whole family gathered on the one night the special aired. Miss it and you waited a full year. TV Guide was circled in advance. | facts: *A Charlie Brown Christmas* aired in 1965 (Wisconsin Hist. Museum). **(unverified)**: the stop-motion reindeer special first aired in 1964 and the animated "Grinch" special in 1966. Home VCRs only spread in the late 1970s–80s. | today: Everything is on demand. The "appointment" feeling is gone. | US/Canada: Canadian families watched these on Canadian or border US stations **(unverified)**. | image: pajama-clad kids sitting close to a wood-cabinet TV, Dad adjusting the rabbit ears, popcorn bowl on the floor.

12. **Calling Santa and the first "Santa tracking," 1955** — details: kids heard the radio say the military was tracking Santa's sleigh on its radar. | facts: In 1955 a Colorado Springs newspaper ad from a department store invited kids to phone Santa, but the number was misprinted. It reached the Continental Air Defense Command (CONAD) center, and the officers went along with it. On Christmas Eve staff put Santa on the tracking board, and the command told the press it was tracking his sleigh. It has been done every year since 1955 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NORAD_Tracks_Santa; cesium.com). Wikipedia notes the story grew over time, and apparently only one child called that night. | today: An app and website with millions of users. | US/Canada: NORAD is a joint US–Canadian command, so Canadian kids grew up with it too (well-known fact; no page checked). | image: a 1960s kitchen wall phone with a curly cord, a small boy on tiptoe holding the receiver, Mom smiling in the doorway.

13. **The 4-cent Christmas stamp and cards everywhere** — details: a stack of cards to address at the kitchen table, licking stamps. The cards that came in were taped around door frames or hung on strings across the living room. | facts: The first US Christmas stamp came out November 1, 1962, in Pittsburgh. It cost 4¢ and showed a wreath and two candles. 500 million were ordered, it sold out, and nearly 862 million were printed by year's end. The 1963 Christmas stamp was 5¢ (about.usps.com). | today: E-cards and social media. Card volumes are lower **(unverified figures)**. | US/Canada: Canada issued what is called the world's first Christmas stamp in 1898, then none until 1964 (info.mysticstamp.com). Canadian postage rates in the 1960s **(unverified)**. | image: a kitchen table at night with a stack of envelopes, a roll of stamps, a fountain pen and a shoebox address book, and cards hung on red yarn behind.

14. **Writing to Santa and getting a reply (Canada)** — details: a letter in crayon addressed "Santa, North Pole," then the thrill of an answer in the mailbox. | facts: In the early 1970s, Montreal postal workers started answering Santa letters on their own time. The earliest documented replies date to 1974, with similar efforts in Vancouver. The official national program came in 1982 (farandwide.beehiiv.com; activeparents.ca; cbc.ca/archives). | today: An official program handling more than a million letters (cbc.ca). | US/Canada: **A Canadian memory, best for viewers born in the mid-60s.** US post office volunteer answering began much earlier **(unverified)**. | image: a girl at a table licking an envelope addressed in crayon, with snow outside the frosted window.

15. **The Santa Claus Parade (Toronto and beyond)** — details: standing on the curb in snow boots, waiting for Santa's float, with marching bands and giant characters. | facts: Eaton's started the Santa Claus Parade in 1905. Santa first rode a horse-drawn cart (heritagetrust.on.ca). Department stores sponsored it to draw Christmas shoppers downtown (museedelaguerre.ca). It was copied in other Canadian cities (activehistory.ca). Whether it was televised in the 1960s **(unverified)**. | today: It still runs but is no longer store-sponsored **(unverified)**. | US/Canada: Canadian-led. US cities had their own store parades (activehistory.ca mentions North American department-store parades in general). | image: bundled-up families on a downtown curb in November light, a child on Dad's shoulders, a float with a waving Santa approaching.

16. **Visiting Santa at the department store** — details: dressing up, waiting in a long line, sitting on Santa's knee. You might get a small gift or candy cane and a black-and-white photo. | facts: The Santa-in-the-toy-department tradition goes back at least to 1903 at Eaton's (heritagetrust.on.ca). 1960s US details (photo prices, store windows) **(unverified)**. | today: Mall Santas with digital photo packages and booked time slots. | US/Canada: both countries. | image: a boy in a clip-on tie on a velvet-suited Santa's knee, Mom holding his coat, fake snow and a toy train in the background.

17. **Spray snow, window stencils and flocked trees** — details: aerosol "snow" sprayed through cardboard stencils onto windows (stars, bells, "Noel"). It smelled strong and took scrubbing in January. Some trees were flocked white. | facts: **(unverified)**: aerosol snow and home flocking kits were common in the 1950s–60s. | today: Window clings and pre-flocked artificial trees. | US/Canada: none known. | image: a mother and daughter at a picture window, the girl holding a stencil while Mom sprays white snow, a winter street outside.

18. **Glowing plastic lawn figures** — details: hollow plastic Santas, snowmen and carolers lit from inside by one bulb. They glowed on the porch or front lawn and blew over in the wind. | facts: **(unverified)**: blow-molded light-up figures spread in the 1960s–70s. | today: Inflatables and LED projectors. Originals are collected. | US/Canada: none known. | image: a ranch house at night, a glowing Santa and snowman on a snowy lawn, big colored bulbs along the eaves.

19. **The bank Christmas Club** — details: a coupon book and a small deposit every week all year. The check came in November just in time for shopping. | facts: **(unverified)**: Christmas Club accounts were common at banks and credit unions. Weekly deposits were often 50¢–$5. They paid little or no interest. | today: Credit cards and buy-now-pay-later. Few banks still offer them. | US/Canada: Canadian versions **(unverified)**. | image: a woman at a marble bank counter handing over a coupon book with a few bills, a wreath on the teller's window.

20. **Flashcubes and the blinding home-movie light** — details: Christmas morning with spinning flashcubes, kids seeing spots, and Dad's movie camera light bar making everyone squint. Slides and photos came back a week later from the drugstore. | facts: **(unverified)**: the flashcube came out around 1965, as did the Super 8 home-movie format. Processing took days. | today: Phone cameras with instant results. | US/Canada: none. | image: kids by the tree in robes shielding their eyes from a bright light bar held by a father, with torn wrapping paper on the floor.

21. **An orange in the toe of the stocking** — details: a real orange, walnuts and filberts in the shell with a nutcracker, ribbon candy stuck together in its dish, and a candy cane. | facts: **(unverified)**: the orange goes back to times when fresh citrus was a winter luxury, and parents who grew up in the Depression kept the habit going. | today: Stockings are filled with gift cards and small electronics. | US/Canada: both countries; the Canadian Christmas orange is often linked to Japanese mandarin shipments **(unverified)**. | image: a red felt stocking with a name in glitter, an orange peeking out of the top, and a bowl of nuts and a nutcracker beside it.

22. **"Batteries not included" and the Christmas Eve assembly** — details: Dad on the floor at midnight with a screwdriver, a dozen parts and instructions he couldn't follow. On Christmas morning a toy wouldn't run because nobody had batteries. | facts: **(unverified)**: battery-powered toys spread in the 1960s, and stores were closed on Christmas Day. | today: Rechargeable toys and 24-hour stores. | US/Canada: none. | image: a father in a cardigan on the living room floor at night with a half-built toy, parts spread out, and a single lamp lit.

23. **Saving the wrapping paper** — details: Grandma carefully unfolded the paper, smoothed it out and saved it with the bows for next year. Gifts came in department-store boxes with tissue. | facts: **(unverified)**: a habit common among families who grew up in the Depression. Peel-and-stick bows spread later. | today: Gift bags and paper that gets thrown away. | US/Canada: none. | image: grandma folding flat a sheet of patterned paper while kids tear into theirs, with a bag of saved bows next to her chair.

24. **A real tree from the lot, and watering it every day** — details: picking a tree in the cold on a gas-station or church lot. It was tied to the car roof and set in a cast-iron stand. The whole house smelled of pine, and needles turned up for months. | facts: **(unverified)**: 1960s prices and tree-lot customs; fire-safety warnings about dry trees and hot bulbs were common in the press. | today: Pre-lit artificial trees and LED lights cut the fire risk. | US/Canada: Canada is a big tree exporter (Nova Scotia, Quebec) **(unverified)**. | image: a father and son carrying a fir through a snowy lot strung with bare bulbs, with a station wagon waiting.

25. **The long-distance call to Grandma** — details: everyone lined up for a turn on the phone. They talked fast because of the cost. "Merry Christmas, can you hear me?" with the cord stretched across the kitchen. | facts: **(unverified)**: long-distance was charged by the minute and was expensive. Holiday lines were busy. | today: Free video calls. | US/Canada: cross-border calls cost even more **(unverified)**. | image: three kids crowding around a kitchen wall phone, the youngest standing on a chair to reach the receiver.

26. **Trading stamps turned into presents** — details: licking stamps into booklets all year, then trading them at the redemption center for a toaster, a lamp or a toy. | facts: **(unverified)**: stamp redemption peaked in the 1960s. | today: Loyalty apps and points. | US/Canada: Canadian versions **(unverified)**. | image: a mother and daughter at a redemption center counter with stacks of filled booklets, shelves of appliances behind them.

27. **School holiday pageant and paper-chain garlands** — details: construction-paper chains glued with paste, popcorn-and-cranberry strings, and the class holiday concert with nervous kids in their best clothes. | facts: **(unverified)**. Keep the focus on crafts and costumes and steer clear of religious content. | today: Many schools hold "winter concerts" instead. | US/Canada: both. | image: a classroom with radiators and tall windows, kids gluing paper chains at desks, a paper snowflake on the window.
