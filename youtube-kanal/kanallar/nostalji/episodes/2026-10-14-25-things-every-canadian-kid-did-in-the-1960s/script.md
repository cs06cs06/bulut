# Growing Up in 1960s Canada: 15 Things Kids Today Won't Believe

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Saturday night. Kids in pyjamas, begging for one more period. Fifteen things kids today won't believe. And number one? Almost every Canadian dad did it on freezing winter nights, and today, in a lot of towns, the weather won't even let you.

## #15 Snowsuits, Toques and Mitts on a String

**s02 · photos**

Before you could go outside, there was the snowsuit. Thick wool or nylon, zipped to the chin. You waddled like a penguin. Your mittens hung on a string threaded through both sleeves, so you couldn't lose them even if you tried. A toque pulled down to your eyebrows. Rubber boots or buckle galoshes. By recess, your scarf was crusted with ice from your own breath. Then everything went on the radiator, and the whole house smelled of wet wool. Americans called it a stocking cap. We knew better. It was a toque, and Mom made sure you wore it.

## #14 Dominion Day Picnics on July First

**s03 · photos**

On July first, we didn't say Canada Day. We said Dominion Day. That's what the holiday was called until nineteen eighty-two. It meant a picnic blanket in the park, a cooler full of sandwiches, and potato salad going warm in the sun. In a lot of small towns there was a parade, with fire trucks and kids on decorated bikes. Then you waited. And waited. Because the fireworks didn't start until it was finally dark, and in a Canadian July, that's late. Ask a grandchild what Dominion Day was. Chances are, they've never heard the name.

## #13 Milk Bottles Clinking on the Porch

**s04 · photos**

Remember the sound of glass bottles clinking on the porch before anyone was up? In a lot of neighbourhoods, the milkman came at dawn. Canadian milk came in heavy, reusable glass bottles, and the cream rose right to the top. In winter, if nobody brought it in fast enough, the milk froze and pushed the cap straight up like a little hat. Then, in nineteen sixty-seven, something new arrived. Milk in plastic bags. Americans still find that strange. But in Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes, plenty of kitchens still have that little pitcher waiting for the bag.

## #12 Canadian Tire Money in the Drawer

**s05 · photos**

Every family had a drawer. Elastic bands, string, a dead battery, and a thick wad of Canadian Tire money. Those little paper bills came with your change, starting at the gas bars in nineteen fifty-eight and in the stores from nineteen sixty-one. Kids treated them like real treasure. You'd count them at the kitchen table, dreaming of a new hockey stick or a bell for your bike. Dad pretended not to notice when a few went missing. The paper bills stopped in twenty twenty. Today it's points on a phone. Somehow, that's not nearly as fun to count.

## #11 The Corner Store Candy Counter

**s06 · photos**

The bell over the door. That's how every trip began. In Quebec it was the dépanneur. Elsewhere it was the variety store or the confectionery. Inside, glass jars and open trays of penny candy, right at eye level. You'd stand there forever, pointing. One of those, two of these. And the owner waited, patient as a saint, filling a tiny paper bag. There was a freezer full of popsicles and a rack of comic books you weren't supposed to read without buying. Today, the Canadian penny itself is gone. Imagine explaining penny candy to a kid now.

## #10 Miles, Pounds and Fahrenheit

**s07 · photos**

We grew up imperial. Gas by the gallon. Weather in Fahrenheit. Rulers marked in inches. And our gallon was bigger than the American one, which made for some confusing road trips south. Then everything changed. On April first, nineteen seventy-five, weather forecasts switched to Celsius. It sounded like an April Fool's joke. Over the Labour Day weekend of nineteen seventy-seven, every speed limit sign in the country went from miles to kilometres per hour. So we learned one system, and our kids learned another. And the Americans? They never switched at all.

## #9 The Orange UNICEF Box at Halloween

**s08 · photos**

Halloween meant a pillowcase in one hand and a little orange box in the other. Trick or treat for UNICEF. Every house gave you candy, and maybe a few pennies dropped in that rattling cardboard box. The first Canadian campaign was in nineteen fifty-five. Schoolchildren raised fifteen thousand dollars that year. Think about that. Kids in homemade costumes, ghosts made from old bedsheets, collecting coins for children they'd never meet. The boxes were retired in two thousand six. But plenty of us can still hear that rattle on a cold porch, under a leaf-covered maple. If these memories brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another slow trip back to the sixties together.

## #8 Polio Vaccine on a Sugar Cube

**s09 · photos**

Most of us remember lining up for a needle. But for polio, it was different. In nineteen sixty-two, Connaught Laboratories in Toronto made an oral polio vaccine. Field trials ran in Quebec City, Montreal and Prince Albert, Saskatchewan. And how did they give it? On a spoon, or dropped on a sugar cube. You stood in line at school or the clinic, braced for the worst, and the nurse handed you sugar. Our parents had lived through real polio fear. So for them, that little cube meant everything. For us, it was the best shot we ever got.

## #7 The Strap Down the Hall

**s10 · photos**

Some memories aren't so sweet. In a lot of schools, getting sent to the principal's office could mean the strap. You walked down that long, polished hallway alone. The whole class went quiet. Nobody needed to say a word. It sounds hard to believe now, but it was simply part of school back then. The Toronto Board of Education banned it in nineteen seventy-one, the first board in Ontario to do so. In other places it lasted much longer, until the Supreme Court ended it in every school in two thousand four. Today, it's unthinkable. And that's a good thing.

## #6 Hunting for the Centennial Animal Coins

**s11 · photos**

In nineteen sixty-seven, even the change in your pocket was special. For Canada's centennial, a Canadian painter designed a new set of coins. A dove on the penny. A rabbit on the nickel. A mackerel on the dime. A bobcat on the quarter. A howling wolf on the fifty-cent piece. And a Canada goose on the dollar, the last silver dollar Canada ever put into circulation. Kids went hunting. Every trip to the store, you checked your change. That rabbit nickel went straight into a jar. A lot of those jars are still sitting in basements today.

## #5 Circling Toys in the Wish Book

**s12 · photos**

Every fall, it landed with a thump. The Eaton's Christmas catalogue. We called it the wish book. Kids sprawled on the living room rug, turning straight to the toy pages, dog-earing every corner. You wrote your initials next to what you wanted, so your brother couldn't claim it. Eaton's sold almost anything by mail. You could even order a house. The catalogue ran from eighteen eighty-four, and the end was announced in January nineteen seventy-six. Americans had their own catalogue. But for us, the wish book was how Christmas really started.

## #4 The Big Bus Trip to Expo 67

**s13 · photos**

Expo sixty-seven. If you were a kid in eastern Canada, you probably begged to go. And a lot of us went by school bus. Early start. Bagged lunch on your lap. A long ride, and then there it was. The future, rising out of the river in Montreal. More than nine hundred and thirty-five thousand schoolchildren came on planned school trips. In Coaticook, Quebec, the town raised money so every child could go, at a dollar fifty a head. The fair drew over fifty million visits. Canada has never hosted a world's fair that big again.

## #3 Getting Your Expo Passport Stamped

**s14 · photos**

And once you were inside, there was the passport. It looked just like a real one. Every pavilion had its own stamp, and you wanted them all. You'd line up for ages just to get one more. Then compare with your friends on the bus home. A child's daily pass cost a dollar twenty-five. A week was six dollars. A whole season, seventeen fifty. Babies under two got in free. The covers were colour-coded, and a child's season pass was red with silver print. Today, people buy those old passports online. Ours? Still in a shoebox, every stamp intact.

## #2 Hockey Night in Canada on Saturdays

**s15 · photos**

Saturday night. Bath done, pyjamas on, and the whole family around the TV. Then that theme song. Plenty of folks called it Canada's second national anthem. In the mid-sixties, those Saturday games were the most popular programs in the country. If you were lucky, you got to stay up for one period. Maybe two, if you were very quiet. Between periods, the grown-ups on screen argued about the game, and so did Dad. In March nineteen sixty-five, the first game went out in colour. And in nineteen sixty-nine, six point two million Canadians watched the Stanley Cup final.

## #1 Dad Flooding the Backyard Rink

**s16 · photos**

And number one. The backyard rink. On the coldest nights, Dad went out after supper with the garden hose. Steam rising under the porch light. Layer after layer, night after night, until it was smooth as glass. Snowbanks were the boards. Boots were the goalposts. We played shinny until we couldn't see the puck, and Mom had to call us in three times. Then frozen toes by the heat register, stinging back to life. Today, in a lot of towns, milder winters make natural ice hard to keep. Toronto now runs fifty-four refrigerated outdoor rinks. But nothing beats ice your own dad made.
