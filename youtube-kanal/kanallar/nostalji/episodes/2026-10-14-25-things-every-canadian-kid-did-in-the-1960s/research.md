# Research: 15 Things Every Canadian Kid Did in the 1960s

> **Status:** I ran out of search and page-fetch quota partway through, so only some items could be checked against sources. **VERIFIED** means a source below confirms it. **UNVERIFIED** means it comes from general knowledge or shared memory, and the script should not state it as fact without checking it first. Sensory details like smells and sounds are typical memories, not documented facts.

## Candidate items

1. **Flooding the backyard rink and playing shinny until dark (VERIFIED in part)**
   - **Details:** Dad flooding the yard with a garden hose on freezing nights. Snowbanks for boards, boots for goalposts. Skates scraping, a puck hitting the back fence, Mom calling everyone in for supper. Frozen toes thawing by the heat register.
   - **Facts:** Backyard rinks are a long-running Canadian winter tradition, and shinny means pickup hockey (cbc.ca Oakville article; Globe and Mail 2015). Warmer winters now make natural ice hard to keep through the season. One Sherbrooke, Que. family found their childhood natural ice "no more" (Globe and Mail, "When backyard dreams melt"). Toronto now runs 54 outdoor rinks with refrigeration (Wikipedia "Shinny").
   - **Today:** Shorter, milder winters. Organized, scheduled hockey and refrigerated city rinks.
   - **US/Canada:** Also common in Minnesota and New England. Far more central to Canadian identity.
   - **Image idea:** Dusk in a snowy backyard. A father in a plaid jacket sprays a hose over the ice under a porch light while kids in toques wait with sticks.

2. **Hockey Night in Canada every Saturday night (VERIFIED)**
   - **Details:** The family gathered around the TV in the living room. Kids in pyjamas allowed to stay up "for one period." Between periods, commentators argued about the game in the "Hot Stove" segment.
   - **Facts:**
     - First French-language TV game Oct 11, 1952 (Montreal). First English broadcast Nov 1, 1952 (Toronto).
     - In 1952 the broadcast came on at 9:30 p.m. ET, one hour after the opening faceoff.
     - Carried across the country from 1957.
     - In the mid-1960s the Saturday games were Canada's most popular programs.
     - First game in colour: March 24, 1965. All games in colour by the start of the 1966–67 season.
     - 6.2 million Canadians watched the 1969 Stanley Cup final.
     - The theme song was seen by many as "Canada's second national anthem."
     - Source: thecanadianencyclopedia.ca; broadcasting-history.ca.
   - **Today:** Rogers bought national NHL rights in Nov 2013. The Canadian Encyclopedia's summary now describes HNIC in the past tense, as airing "from October 1952 until June 2026." **Verify that end date before using it.**
   - **US/Canada:** Uniquely Canadian. American kids had no national Saturday hockey ritual.
   - **Image idea:** A 1960s living room. A black-and-white console TV glows while a father in a cardigan and two kids in flannel pyjamas sit on the carpet. Bowl of popcorn, lamp light.

3. **The snowsuit, mitts on a string, and the toque (UNVERIFIED)**
   - **Details:** Bulky wool or nylon snowsuits that made you waddle. Mittens joined by a string running through the sleeves. Rubber boots or buckle galoshes. Scarves crusted with ice from your breath. Wet wool steaming on the radiator.
   - **Facts:** No sources found this session. "Toque" is a Canadian word.
   - **Today:** Lighter synthetic insulation, waterproof boots, clip-on mitten holders.
   - **US/Canada:** Americans say "stocking cap" or "knit hat." "Toque" is a Canadian marker.
   - **Image idea:** A school mudroom with rows of dripping snowsuits on hooks, mittens drying on a radiator, and a small child being zipped up by her mother.

4. **The corner store and penny candy (UNVERIFIED prices)**
   - **Details:** A bell over the door. Glass jars and open trays of one-cent candies, a small paper bag, the owner waiting patiently while you chose. Comic book racks, freezer chest of popsicles.
   - **Facts:** Penny candy and chocolate bar prices of about 10¢ in the 1960s are unverified. **Do not quote prices.**
   - **Today:** Corner stores are fewer. The Canadian penny was taken out of circulation, which UNICEF Canada cites as one reason it ended its coin boxes (unicef.ca).
   - **US/Canada:** In Quebec the corner store is the "dépanneur." Elsewhere it is the "variety store" or "confectionery."
   - **Image idea:** A child on tiptoe at a wooden counter holding up coins while a shopkeeper fills a paper bag. Sunlight through a window crowded with signs.

5. **Returning pop bottles for the deposit (UNVERIFIED amounts)**
   - **Details:** Kids collecting empties from ditches and garages and hauling them in a wagon to the store to trade for candy money.
   - **Facts:** Deposit amounts and rules varied by province and are not verified.
   - **Today:** Plastic and cans. Deposit systems now run provincially.
   - **US/Canada:** Common in both countries.
   - **Image idea:** Two kids pulling a red wagon clinking with glass bottles along a gravel road.

6. **The school bus trip to Expo 67 (VERIFIED)**
   - **Details:** An early start, bagged lunches, a long bus ride, first sight of the futuristic pavilions.
   - **Facts:**
     - Open from late April to the end of October 1967.
     - Total attendance was 50,306,648, second only to Paris in 1900.
     - 935,631 schoolchildren came on planned school trips, about 50,000 of them from the Ottawa area.
     - Coaticook, Que. raised money locally to cover a $1.50-per-child trip cost.
     - Sources: expo67.ncf.ca; 100objects.qahn.org.
   - **Today:** No world's fair of that scale has been held in Canada since. Montreal school trips are now museum outings.
   - **US/Canada:** Many American families drove up to see it.
   - **Image idea:** Kids in 1967 clothes pressing their faces to bus windows as a geodesic dome comes into view on a bright summer day.

7. **Getting your Expo "passport" stamped at every pavilion (VERIFIED)**
   - **Details:** A booklet that looked like a real passport, collecting a stamp from each "country," and comparing stamps with friends.
   - **Facts:**
     - Prices: adults $2.50 per day, $12 per week, $35 per season. Children $1.25 per day, $6 per week, $17.50 per season.
     - Age groups: children 2–12, youth 13–21, adults over 21. Under-2s got in free.
     - Covers were colour-coded. For example, the child season pass had a red cover with silver print.
     - Sources: expo67.ncf.ca; canadashistory.ca; QAHN.
   - **Today:** Old passports sell on eBay for roughly C$28–75.
   - **Image idea:** Close-up of a child's hands holding an open passport full of colourful stamps, with a pavilion line-up blurred behind.

8. **Finding a centennial animal coin in your change (VERIFIED)**
   - **Details:** Kids hunting for the rabbit nickel or the wolf half-dollar and saving them in jars.
   - **Facts:**
     - The 1967 coins were designed by a Canadian painter.
     - Designs: dove on the 1¢, rabbit on the 5¢, mackerel on the 10¢, bobcat on the 25¢, howling wolf on the 50¢, Canada goose on the $1.
     - The 1967 goose dollar was the last circulating Canadian silver dollar.
     - Sources: aci-iac.ca; samwallermuseum.ca; pcgs.com.
     - The switch of dimes and quarters to nickel in 1968 is UNVERIFIED.
   - **Today:** Pennies are gone, and $1 and $2 coins replaced the bills.
   - **US/Canada:** Silver coins disappeared in the US in 1965, a few years earlier.
   - **Image idea:** A child's palm holding a rabbit nickel next to a glass jar of coins on a kitchen table.

9. **Centennial Year fever, 1967 (UNVERIFIED details)**
   - **Details:** Centennial projects in every town, the catchy "Ca-na-da" song, centennial medallions handed out at school, the travelling Confederation Train.
   - **Facts:** I could not fetch a source. **Verify all of these before use.**
   - **Today:** Canada 150 in 2017 was the closest echo.
   - **Image idea:** A small-town parade with kids waving paper flags and a banner reading "1867–1967."

10. **Milk delivered in glass bottles, then the first milk bags (VERIFIED in part)**
    - **Details:** Bottles clinking on the porch at dawn. Cream on top. Frozen milk pushing up the cap in winter.
    - **Facts:** Canadian milk came in heavy, reusable glass bottles in imperial sizes, plus cartons and jugs. In 1967 a chemical company brought plastic milk bags to Canada using European equipment. Bags spread when metric conversion in the 1970s forced bottles, jugs and cartons to be redesigned, while bag machines were easy to resize (Wikipedia "Milk bag"). The details of home delivery are UNVERIFIED.
    - **Today:** Bags are still common in Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes. Home delivery is mostly gone.
    - **US/Canada:** Milk bags are a strongly Canadian difference. Americans rarely saw them.
    - **Image idea:** Early morning on a front step. A milk crate with glass bottles, frost on the railing, a child's hand reaching out.

11. **Stashing Canadian Tire money in the kitchen drawer (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Small paper "bills" handed over with your change, saved up for a hockey stick or bike part.
    - **Facts:** Introduced in 1958 at the company's gas bars and from 1961 in its retail stores (DCHP, UBC). Denominations ran from 5¢ to $2, with a cartoon Scotsman mascot (vintagegifts.ca, weaker source). It went digital in 2012, and paper issue ended in 2020 (Wikipedia).
    - **Today:** Digital loyalty points.
    - **US/Canada:** Canada only.
    - **Image idea:** An open kitchen drawer stuffed with small paper bills, elastic bands and string. A child counting the bills at the table.

12. **Circling toys in the Eaton's Christmas "wish book" (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** The catalogue arriving in autumn, the toy pages dog-eared, initials written next to the wanted items.
    - **Facts:** Published 1884–1976. The Spring/Summer 1976 catalogue was the last one mailed. Its end was announced Jan 14, 1976. It was nicknamed "the wish book" at Christmas. You could order almost anything by mail, even a house. Mail-order offices were in Toronto, Winnipeg (1905) and Moncton (1918). Sources: rcinet.ca; Wikipedia; ourontario.ca. The Simpsons-Sears wish book is UNVERIFIED.
    - **Today:** Online shopping.
    - **US/Canada:** Americans had the Sears catalogue. Eaton's was Canada's own.
    - **Image idea:** Two siblings on their stomachs on a braided rug, a thick catalogue open, a pencil, a Christmas tree glowing behind them.

13. **The strap in the principal's office (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Being sent down the hall, the leather strap, the whole class going quiet.
    - **Facts:** The Toronto Board of Education banned the strap in 1971, the first board in Ontario to do so. Elsewhere in Canada it stayed in use until the 1990s. The Supreme Court ended it in all schools on Jan 30, 2004. Sources: edcan.ca; ERIC; Project MUSE.
    - **Today:** Unthinkable.
    - **Tone note:** Handle gently.
    - **Image idea:** An empty 1960s school hallway with polished floors, wooden benches outside a frosted-glass office door, afternoon light.

14. **Miles, pounds and Fahrenheit (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Gas sold by the gallon, weather in °F, rulers in inches.
    - **Facts:**
      - The Metric Commission was set up in 1971.
      - Weather forecasts switched to Celsius on April 1, 1975.
      - Rainfall in mm and snowfall in cm from 1975.
      - Every speed limit sign changed from mph to km/h over the Labour Day weekend of 1977.
      - Before metric, Canadian gas was sold by the imperial gallon (about 4.55 L), versus the US gallon (3.79 L).
      - Sources: Wikipedia "Metrication in Canada"; statcan.gc.ca; globalnews.ca.
    - **Today:** Kids of the 1960s grew up imperial, while their own children learned metric.
    - **US/Canada:** The US never converted, which makes this a strong Canadian contrast.
    - **Image idea:** A 1960s gas station with an attendant at a pump showing gallons and a station wagon full of kids.

15. **Dominion Day on July 1 (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Picnics, small-town parades, evening fireworks.
    - **Facts:** The holiday was called Dominion Day until it was renamed Canada Day in 1982 (thecanadianencyclopedia.ca; cbc.ca archives).
    - **Today:** The name is forgotten by younger Canadians.
    - **Image idea:** A family picnic on a park blanket with a cooler and paper flags, fireworks starting over a lake.

16. **Collecting for UNICEF with the orange box at Halloween (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Pillowcase for candy in one hand, little orange cardboard box rattling with pennies in the other.
    - **Facts:** The first Canadian campaign was in 1955, and schoolchildren raised $15,000 that year (trickortreatforunicef.ca; Wikipedia). In later years the boxes raised about $3 million a year, with volunteers rolling coins (cbc.ca; lbbonline). Boxes were retired in Canada in 2006 and the campaign went digital in 2019 (cbc.ca; unicef.ca).
    - **US/Canada:** It began in the US in 1950.
    - **Image idea:** Kids in homemade costumes on a leaf-covered porch, one holding up an orange box to a smiling neighbour.

17. **Polio vaccine on a sugar cube (VERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Lining up at school or a clinic and getting a sugar cube instead of a needle.
    - **Facts:** Connaught Laboratories in Toronto produced the oral polio vaccine in 1962. Field trials were held in Quebec City, Montreal and Prince Albert, Sask. The vaccine was given by spoon or dropped on a sugar cube (connaught.research.utoronto.ca). Canada's later switch to injected vaccine is UNVERIFIED.
    - **US/Canada:** The US had "Sabin Sundays" in 1960 (uc.edu).
    - **Image idea:** A nurse in a white cap offering a sugar cube on a spoon to a toddler held by his mother.

18. **CBC children's TV after school (UNVERIFIED)**
    - **Details:** Rabbit-ear antenna, one or two channels, gentle hosts, puppets, a "rocking chair" opening.
    - **Facts:** Show names and start years (for example, programs launched in 1958 and 1967) are unverified. Avoid naming hosts.
    - **Image idea:** A child sitting cross-legged close to a small TV set, afternoon sun on the carpet.

19. **Wildlife clips with the loon call and flute (UNVERIFIED)**

    - **Image idea:** Mist on a northern lake at dawn, a loon on the water, a cottage dock in the foreground.
