# Research: 15 Things Every Family Did on Thanksgiving Day in the 1970s — Unthinkable Today

## Candidate items

**Hour-by-hour structure (suggested):** before dawn → morning parade → arrivals → afternoon football → 3–4 pm dinner → evening slump → the day after.

1. **The bird went in before sunrise** — Grandma in her housecoat creeping downstairs around 5 a.m. to start the turkey. Mom had been up since dawn stirring gravy, mashing potatoes and baking pies. The cooking took all day because people were afraid of undercooking the bird. | Pop-up turkey timers were used in turkeys and roasting chickens from about 1965 (K-State Extension; MSU Extension). The patent for the "thermally responsive signaling device" was granted Oct 25, 1966 (Cade Museum). The common timer is set to about 180°F, while the safe temperature is 165°F (ThermoWorks blog), so 1970s birds were often overcooked. A first-person memory of the 5 a.m. start is in a HuffPost piece. | Today: cheap digital thermometers, recipes online, and many people cook a breast or spatchcocked bird faster. | US/Canada: same practice. | Image: dark kitchen at 5 a.m., one bulb over the stove, a woman in a quilted housecoat sliding a roasting pan into a harvest-gold oven, frost on the window.

2. **Turkey was a real splurge** — the bird was the costliest thing in the cart. | BLS retail turkey averaged **89.6¢/lb in Nov 1973** (the year grocery prices rose about 21%) and **77.4¢/lb in Nov 1975**. A 16-lb bird cost about **$12.38 in 1975**, which was about **2½ hours of pay** at the average production worker's $4.86/hr. In 2025 a turkey took about 40 minutes of pay (Mental Floss, citing BLS/USDA). | Today: inflation-adjusted, turkey costs much less per pound. There was no national "Thanksgiving dinner cost" survey until 1986 (Mental Floss). | US figures only; I found no Canadian prices (unverified). | Image: butcher-paper price sign "Turkeys 77¢ lb" over a grocery freezer case, a woman in a wool coat checking her wallet.

3. **Smaller birds** — family turkeys were more modest. | Before the 1970s, US turkeys averaged about **15 lb per bird**. Today it's over 30 lb (Jayson Lusk). USDA NASS gives 30.3 lb average in 2013. *Caution: these are industry-wide live weights, not supermarket birds.* | Today: decades of selective breeding made birds much heavier. | — | Image: a 1970s cookbook page showing a modest bird on a platter.

4. **No turkey hotline and no internet. You phoned Mom.** | The first big consumer turkey hotline opened only in **1981**: six home economists answered about 11,000 calls that season (AJC; company "About Us" page). Name the hotline generically, without the brand. | Today: videos, apps, text lines. | — | Image: a young wife on a yellow wall phone with the cord stretched across the kitchen, flour on her hands, a recipe card propped against the sugar canister.

5. **The parade on the console TV** — kids sprawled on the carpet in pajamas while balloons floated by, black-and-white sets in older homes and color in luckier ones (Brooklyn Hall of Fame memoir). | NBC carried the New York parade every year. Its hosts in 1963–72 were a western-series actor and a comedienne (Wikipedia; don't name them, per channel rules). | Today: streaming, replays, phone screens. | US/Canada: **unverified (forum source)**: a radio-history forum says CBS ran a rival multi-city parade broadcast from about 9 a.m. to noon in the mid-1970s, including **Toronto**. | Image: four kids on shag carpet in front of a wood-cabinet console TV with rabbit ears, a giant balloon on screen, morning light through sheers.

6. **Santa at the end of the parade officially opened Christmas** | An archive notes the final minutes of a 1962 broadcast "including arrival of Santa Claus" (Archival Television Audio). The idea that nobody decorated before Santa arrived is **unverified**, though widely remembered. | Today: Christmas displays go up before Halloween. | Canada: Toronto's own Santa Claus parade is in November (**unverified**). | Image: a family pointing at Santa's sleigh float on a TV screen, a toddler clapping.

7. **Two networks, two parades, one TV — and someone had to get up and turn the dial** (**unverified**: per the forum, NBC ran about 10 a.m.–noon in 1973–75 and 9 a.m.–noon in 1977–78, against CBS from 9 to noon). | Today: remotes and DVRs. | — | Image: a boy kneeling at the set, hand on the click-dial, Dad pointing from his chair.

8. **Everyone in their Sunday best** — pressed pants, dresses and stiff shoes for the drive to Grandma's. The good tablecloth, special dishes and serving platters came out (Cheapism, 2026). | Today: jeans and sweaters. | — | Image: a family on a front porch in November: a boy in a clip-on tie, a girl in a plaid jumper and patent-leather shoes, Mom in a pantsuit holding a covered casserole.

9. **The car trip to Grandma's** — the week of Thanksgiving, a family drove "up north" to stay at the grandparents' house (HuffPost memoir, Rochester NY). Kids loose in the back seat, often sliding around. | **Unverified:** US state seat-belt laws mostly came in the mid-1980s, and Ontario's arrived around 1976. Check both before stating. | Today: car seats, belt laws, GPS. | US/Canada: Canadian provinces likely legislated earlier (**unverified**). | Image: a wood-paneled station wagon on a gray two-lane highway, bare trees, kids' faces pressed to the rear window, a pie on Mom's lap.

10. **Ashtrays in the living room, smoke over the parade** — glass ashtrays on the coffee table and relatives lighting up indoors. A collector's story describes a green glass ashtray saved from use at a Thanksgiving gathering (Collectors Weekly). | Adult smoking: **36.7% in 1970** (CDC/NCHS, ages 17+). Gallup self-reports: 43% in 1972 and 36% in 1977, with the 1970s average around 40%. Use "roughly 4 in 10 adults." | Today: far fewer smokers, and many homes and all public buildings are smoke-free. | Canada: comparable rates (**unverified**). | Image: an amber glass ashtray beside a candy dish and TV Guide, a curl of smoke, uncles on a plaid sofa.

11. **Football on all afternoon** | The Detroit Lions have hosted on Thanksgiving since 1934 (except 1939–44). Dallas joined in **1966** before 80,259 fans, and has played every year since except **1975 and 1977** (ESPN). Some households timed dinner to the game (Cheapism). | Today: three games, streaming, and dinner rarely built around kickoff. | **Canada:** the CFL plays Monday-afternoon games on Thanksgiving weekend (Wikipedia, Thanksgiving (Canada)). | Image: men leaning toward a TV, one in a cardigan mid-shout, a woman in an apron calling from the doorway.

12. **The cousins' backyard football game** — a patch of grass, a ball, uneven teams and disputed touchdowns. It ended in muddy pressed slacks when someone yelled "wash up!" (Cheapism). | Today: smaller families, more screen time. | — | Image: five boys on a leaf-covered lawn, one in church shoes, a mother at the screen door.

13. **The women ran the kitchen** — handwritten recipe cards, mountains of potatoes, several casseroles fighting for one oven, and relatives arriving with dishes to reheat (Cheapism). | Today: shared cooking, potluck, store-bought sides. | — | Image: three generations of women in a steamy kitchen, the oven door open, recipe cards in a tin box.

14. **The kids' table** — a folding card table in the kitchen or den with mismatched chairs and unbreakable plates. Being promoted to the grown-ups' table was a milestone (Cheapism; a retro food blog). | Today: smaller families, so everyone fits at one table. | — | Image: four kids at a wobbly card table, a girl in pigtails reaching for a roll, the adult table blurred behind them.

15. **The good china (and washing it by hand afterward)** — the dishes and tablecloth that came out only on holidays (Cheapism). | **Unverified:** many homes had no dishwasher, so the women washed and dried in the sink. Check dishwasher-ownership data before stating. | Today: dishwashers, paper plates. | — | Image: two women at the sink, one drying gold-rimmed china with a tea towel, a stack of plates.

16. **Jell-O molds as a "salad"** — bright gelatin with cranberries, pineapple, nuts, marshmallows or even shredded carrots, served alongside the turkey (Cheapism). Say "gelatin" generically. | Today: rarely made, and mostly an ironic novelty when it is. | — | Image: a ring of red gelatin with a whipped-cream center on a cut-glass plate under a dining-room chandelier.

17. **Cranberry sauce with the can ridges still on it** — sliced into neat rounds on a plate (Cheapism). | Conflicting date: a retro blog says canned jellied sauce dates to 1912 (**unverified**; other dates are often cited). Don't state a year. | Today: homemade relish is fashionable again. | — | Image: a ridged ruby cylinder on a small glass dish, a knife beside it.

18. **The 1955 green bean casserole** — canned beans, cream-of-mushroom soup and crispy fried onions, a test-kitchen recipe from **1955** that was standard by the '70s (Cheapism). Avoid the brand name. | Today: still made, often from scratch. | — | Image: a bubbling Pyrex dish fresh from the oven, an oven-mitted hand.

19. **Carving at the table, with an electric knife** — Dad or Grandpa carving at the head of the table. A reader photo from 1965 calls the electric knife "state of the art" (Taste of Home). The wishbone was saved to dry for two kids to pull (Cheapism). | Today: the bird is usually carved in the kitchen. | — | Image: a man in a sweater-vest buzzing an electric knife into a golden turkey, kids watching, the wishbone on a saucer.

20. **Pilgrim hats at the family table** — paper Pilgrim hats worn by siblings at dinner, Albion, Pennsylvania, 1979 (Taste of Home). Kids made them at school (Cheapism). Keep it to Pilgrim hats and handprint turkeys; avoid the "Indian headdress" angle. | Today: schools have reworked these lessons. | — | Image: two kids in black construction-paper Pilgrim hats grinning over their plates.

21. **The days before: handprint turkeys and classroom feasts** — traced-hand turkeys, bulletin boards covered in paper leaves, desks pushed together for a mini feast with moms' homemade cookies and popcorn balls (Cheapism, school traditions). | Today: allergy rules often require packaged snacks. | — | Image: a 1970s classroom, kids gluing paper feathers, a "Happy Thanksgiving" chalkboard.

22. **TV shows only aired once** — an animated holiday special first aired in **1973**. If you missed it, you waited (Cheapism; Easy Everyday Recipes). Teachers wheeled a TV cart into class before the break (Cheapism). Don't name the show or brand. | Today: on demand anytime. | — | Image: kids cross-legged before a wood console TV, a cartoon on screen.

23. **Dad asleep in the recliner** — the day ended with everyone "sprawled across the living room, too full to move" (Cheapism). | The tryptophan explanation for the drowsiness is **unverified**. Treat it as a myth or leave it out. | Today: phones and streaming keep people awake. | — | Image: a man asleep in a brown vinyl recliner, mouth open, football still on TV, a kid tucking an afghan over him.

24. **The after-dinner slide show** — Grandpa's carousel projector clicking through hummingbirds, roses and family shots (HuffPost memoir). | Today: photos on phones, or cast to the TV. | — | Image: a darkened living room, a projector beam full of cigarette smoke (link to #10), a bed-sheet screen, faces lit blue.

25. **Leftover turkey sandwiches for days** — cold turkey on soft white bread with mayo, stuffing or cranberry. Then soup and casseroles (Cheapism). | Today: smaller birds bought for smaller gatherings, and leftovers given away. | — | Image: an open 1970s fridge, a foil-covered platter, a sandwich on a flowered plate.

26. **"Black Friday" was a Philadelphia police complaint, not a holiday** | Philly police used it in the 1950s and '60s for the downtown traffic jams and crowds (History.com). Merchants tried "Big Friday" around 1961 and failed. The term still wasn't common nationwide in **1985** (History.com; Britannica). Stores being closed on Thanksgiving Day itself is **unverified**. | Today: openings on Thanksgiving night, online sales. | **Canada:** Thanksgiving falls in October, so there was no day-after shopping link (**unverified**). | Image: a crowded downtown department-store sidewalk, shoppers in long coats, a traffic cop's whistle.

27. **CANADA BONUS: Thanksgiving in October, on a Monday** | Fixed as the **second Monday of October** by a proclamation on **Jan 31, 1957**. It's not a statutory holiday in PEI, NB or NS (Canadian Encyclopedia). Families may eat on Saturday, Sunday or Monday of the long weekend (Almanac). | Today: unchanged; viewers in each country can compare in the comments. | — | Image: an Ontario farmhouse table by a window with red and orange maples outside.

28. **The Christmas catalog arrived** — kids circled toys on the living-room floor that weekend. **Unverified**: no sources found. Big department-store catalogs were sent out in the fall, and Canada's main one ended in 1976. Verify all of this before use. | Image: two kids on their bellies flipping a thick catalog, felt-tip circles on the toy pages.
