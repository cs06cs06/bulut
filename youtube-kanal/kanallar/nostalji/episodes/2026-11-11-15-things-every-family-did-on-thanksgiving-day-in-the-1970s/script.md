# 15 Thanksgiving Things We All Did in the 1970s — Unthinkable Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Five in the morning, and the turkey is already going in. We'll spend the whole day together, right up to Dad snoring in the recliner. But number one? We all sat there, we all wanted out, and the day we left it, something ended for good.

## #15 The Turkey Went In Before Sunrise

**s02 · photos**

Remember waking up to the smell of sage and onions while it was still dark? Around five in the morning, Mom or Grandma crept downstairs in her quilted housecoat. One bulb burned over the stove. Frost on the window. The roasting pan slid into the oven, and the whole house slowly filled with that smell. Everyone was terrified of an undercooked bird, so it roasted all day. Even those little pop-up timers popped at about one hundred eighty degrees, well past the safe mark. Today, a cheap digital thermometer settles it in seconds. Back then, the turkey came out a little dry, and the gravy did the rest.

## #14 No Hotline, You Called Mom

**s03 · photos**

If the turkey went wrong, there was no website. No video. No app. You picked up the wall phone in the kitchen and called your mother. The cord stretched clear across the room, so you could baste with one hand and listen with the other. Flour on the receiver. A recipe card propped against the sugar canister. The first big turkey hotline didn't open until nineteen eighty-one, and even then, just six home economists answered the phones. In the seventies, the hotline was Mom. And somehow, she always picked up.

## #13 The Parade on the Console TV

**s04 · photos**

By mid-morning, we kids were sprawled on the shag carpet in our pajamas, chins in our hands, a few feet from the big wooden console TV. Rabbit ears on top. Giant balloons drifted between the buildings in New York, and we gasped every time the wind tugged at one. Some homes still had black and white. The lucky ones had color, and you could tell, because the neighbor kids came over. Today, you can watch it on a phone in the car. Back then, you watched it live, together, or not at all.

## #12 Santa Opened the Christmas Season

**s05 · photos**

And the very end of the parade was the part we waited for. Santa Claus, waving from his sleigh. The moment he appeared, something switched on inside every kid in the room. Christmas had officially begun. In a lot of homes, nobody would dream of hanging a wreath or playing a carol before that sleigh rolled by. That was the rule, even if nobody wrote it down. Today, the tinsel goes up next to the Halloween candy. Back then, we waited for Santa to give us permission.

## #11 Everyone in Their Sunday Best

**s06 · photos**

Then came the part we dreaded. Getting dressed. Not play clothes. Sunday best. Pressed slacks with a crease you could slice bread with. A clip-on tie that pinched. Stiff shoes, or shiny patent leather for the girls, with a plaid jumper and tights that itched all day. Mom in a smart pantsuit, holding a covered casserole like the crown jewels. At Grandma's, the good tablecloth and the special platters came out too. Today, folks show up in jeans and sweatpants. Back then, you dressed up for a turkey.

## #10 The Long Drive to Grandma's

**s07 · photos**

Then we piled into the station wagon. Dad at the wheel, a pie on Mom's lap, and three or four kids loose in the back, sliding across the vinyl seat on every curve. Bare trees. Gray skies. A two-lane highway that went on forever. Somebody always asked, are we there yet, before we'd even left town. Lots of families drove for hours and stayed the whole holiday weekend. Today, kids ride buckled into car seats with a screen in front of them. Back then, our entertainment was counting cows and fighting over the window.

## #9 Football All Afternoon

**s08 · photos**

Once the coats came off, the men found the TV. Football. Detroit had played on Thanksgiving for decades, and Dallas joined in nineteen sixty-six, so by the seventies the afternoon belonged to the game. Uncles leaned toward the screen in cardigans, groaning at every fumble. In some houses, dinner was even timed around kickoff. And from the kitchen doorway came the call, it's almost ready, which nobody believed. Up in Canada, the big games came on Thanksgiving Monday in October. Either way, somebody was yelling at a referee who couldn't hear him.

## #8 The Cousins' Backyard Football Game

**s09 · photos**

Meanwhile, out back, the cousins had their own game. A patch of grass, one scuffed ball, and teams that were never even. The biggest cousin always played quarterback. Every touchdown was disputed. Leaves stuck to our church clothes, grass stained our good knees, and somebody's little brother cried and still wanted to play. It lasted until a voice from the screen door shouted, wash up! Then we trooped inside, muddy pressed slacks and all. Today, families are smaller and spread out. Back then, there were enough cousins to field two teams. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the seventies together.

## #7 One Oven, Five Casseroles

**s10 · photos**

Inside, the kitchen was the hottest room in the house, and the women ran it like a command post. Mountains of potatoes. Handwritten recipe cards, splattered and soft from years of use. Aunts arriving with casseroles that all needed the oven at once. The windows steamed up. The green bean casserole with crispy onions on top, a test-kitchen recipe from nineteen fifty-five, was practically required by law. Today, plenty of sides come ready-made from the store. Back then, one oven had to feed twenty people, and somehow it did.

## #6 The Gelatin Salad and Ridged Cranberry

**s11 · photos**

And on the table, right next to the turkey, sat something that wiggled. The gelatin salad. Bright red or lime green, shaped in a ring mold, with cranberries, pineapple, nuts, little marshmallows, and sometimes shredded carrots floating inside like a science project. We called it salad. With a straight face. Beside it, the cranberry sauce slid out of the can in one piece, ridges and all, sliced into neat little rounds. Today, gelatin salad is mostly a joke at retro parties. Back then, it was the star, and it trembled every time someone set down a glass.

## #5 The Electric Knife and the Wishbone

**s12 · photos**

Then the big moment. The turkey came out whole on a platter, and Dad or Grandpa carved it right there at the head of the table. With an electric knife. That buzzing sound was the sound of a holiday. Everybody watched like it was surgery. And the wishbone? That went on a saucer by the window to dry for a few days, until two kids could hook their pinkies around it and pull. Today, the bird usually gets carved in the kitchen, out of sight. Back then, carving was a show, and Dad was the star.

## #4 Dad Asleep in the Recliner

**s13 · photos**

After pie, the house went quiet. The whole family sprawled across the living room, too full to move. And Dad? Dad was in the recliner, feet up, mouth open, snoring through the fourth quarter. One of the kids tucked an afghan over him. The game kept playing to nobody. The grown-ups swore it was something in the turkey. Honestly, it was probably the three helpings and the second slice of pie. Today, everybody's scrolling on a phone. Back then, the loudest sound in the house after dinner was Dad.

## #3 A Turkey Cost Hours of Pay

**s14 · photos**

Here's something our grandchildren won't believe. That turkey was a splurge. In November of nineteen seventy-five, turkey ran about seventy-seven cents a pound in the United States. A sixteen-pound bird came to roughly twelve dollars. Sounds cheap, right? But the average factory worker earned under five dollars an hour. So that bird cost about two and a half hours of work. Today, it's closer to forty minutes. And just two years earlier, grocery prices had jumped about twenty-one percent in a single year. So when Mom said don't waste it, she meant it. That's why we ate turkey sandwiches for a week.

## #2 Ashtrays on the Coffee Table

**s15 · photos**

Now picture the living room that afternoon. Glass ashtrays on the coffee table, amber or green, right next to the candy dish. Uncles on the plaid sofa lighting up during the game. Aunts with a cigarette in one hand and a coffee cup in the other. A blue haze hung under the lamps, and nobody opened a window. In the seventies, roughly four in ten American adults smoked. Grandma didn't send anyone outside. She emptied the ashtrays between courses. Today, one cigarette would clear the room. Back then, the smoke was as much a part of Thanksgiving as the stuffing.

## #1 The Kids' Table

**s16 · photos**

And number one. The place every one of us remembers. The kids' table. A wobbly folding card table in the kitchen or the den, mismatched chairs, and plastic plates that wouldn't break no matter how hard we tried. We spilled milk. We kicked each other underneath. We heard the grown-ups laughing in the next room, and we wanted in so badly. Then one year, somebody pulled out a chair for you at the big table. You'd made it. But you could still hear the cousins giggling without you. Today, smaller families squeeze around one table. Back then, the kids' table was the last place we were all little together.
