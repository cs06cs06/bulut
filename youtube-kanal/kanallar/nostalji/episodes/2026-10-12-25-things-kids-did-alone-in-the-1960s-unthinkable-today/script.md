# 15 Things Kids Did Alone in the 1960s That Are Unthinkable Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Our only curfew was a streetlight. Nobody knew where we were, and that was normal. Here are fifteen things we did alone. And number one? We did it at six, every school day. Wait till you hear how few kids are allowed to do it now.

## #15 Trick-or-Treating With No Grown-Ups

**s02 · photos**

Halloween night, we grabbed a pillowcase off the bed and ran out the door. No parents. Just a pack of kids in bedsheet ghosts and plastic masks that made your face sweat. We covered whole neighborhoods, block after block, porch light after porch light. Some houses handed out popcorn balls or candy apples somebody's grandmother made that afternoon. Up in Canada, half the costumes went on over snowsuits. We came home with a pillowcase so heavy we dragged it up the steps. Today, it's parents with flashlights and a set time to finish. Back then, the night belonged to us.

## #14 Sent to the Corner Store

**s03 · photos**

Mom would scribble a list on the back of an envelope, fold it around some coins, and send you down to the corner store. Milk, a loaf of bread, maybe a can of soup. The bell over the door jingled. The man behind the counter knew your name, and your mother's, and sometimes he'd just say, put it on the account. You carried the bag home in both arms, proud as a delivery man. Plenty of us remember fetching Dad's cigarettes too. Today, few stores would hand a seven-year-old anything without a grown-up. Back then, a note was all the ID you needed.

## #13 Penny Candy, Your Own Money

**s04 · photos**

And if a penny or two was left over? That was yours. The candy counter had rows of glass jars, and you stood there pointing, one at a time. Wax lips. Licorice whips. Jawbreakers that lasted all afternoon. The shopkeeper waited, patient as a saint, while you agonized over every cent and he dropped each piece into a little paper sack. A candy bar ran roughly a nickel early in the decade, closer to a dime by the end. Today, true penny candy is hard to find. Back then, we were kids with two cents and big decisions.

## #12 Riding the City Bus Downtown

**s05 · photos**

In the city, the bus was our bicycle. You tucked the exact fare into your mitten so you couldn't lose it, climbed those tall steps, and dropped the coins in the box with a clink. Off downtown to the library, the dentist, or Grandma's apartment. Ten years old, alone, watching the shops roll past the window. You knew to pull the cord one stop early. In Toronto and Montreal, plenty of kids did the same on streetcars and buses. Today, a ten-year-old riding alone would draw a lot of worried looks. Back then, we just got our library books and rode home.

## #11 A Dime for the Pay Phone

**s06 · photos**

Every kid knew one rule. Keep a dime for the phone. In a lot of places, that's what a local call cost. If you missed the bus or the ball game ran late, you found a phone booth, folded the glass door shut, and stood on tiptoe to reach the slot. The coin dropped with a little ring. You dialed the number you knew by heart, and Mom picked up. Come get me. Today, pay phones have nearly vanished, and kids carry phones in their pockets. Back then, our whole safety plan was one dime, tucked in a shoe.

## #10 Babysitting at Eleven or Twelve

**s07 · photos**

At eleven or twelve, we were the babysitter. A neighbor phoned your mother, and Friday night you walked over with your homework. The phone number was written on a slip by the phone. You warmed a bottle, read the same picture book four times, and wrestled two toddlers into pajamas. Then the best part. The house went quiet, and you had someone else's television all to yourself. When the parents came home, you got paid in cash at the door and walked home under the stars. Today, sitters are usually older, often with a first-aid course. Back then, a twelve-year-old was the grown-up in charge.

## #9 Saturday Matinee, No Adults

**s08 · photos**

Saturday afternoon, Mom handed you some change and pointed you toward the movie theater. No parent came along. One fellow remembers paying fifteen cents for Saturday matinees in the early sixties. Inside, it was a room full of kids and pure noise. Cartoons first, sometimes a serial with the hero dangling off a cliff, then a double feature. The floors were sticky. The popcorn was salty. An usher with a flashlight walked the aisles, trying to keep order and mostly failing. Today, nobody drops a seven-year-old at the movies for the afternoon. Back then, the usher was the only babysitter. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another walk back to the neighborhoods we grew up in.

## #8 Fireworks From the Roadside Stand

**s09 · photos**

Early July, the roadside stands went up, and kids lined up with their allowance. Firecrackers, sparklers, and those punk sticks that glowed orange and smelled like a campfire. We set off strings of firecrackers in the street and sent tin cans hopping off the curb. Somebody always came home with singed eyebrows. In the United States, the really big ones, cherry bombs and M eighties, were banned federally in nineteen sixty-six. Canadian kids had Victoria Day and Dominion Day for their own sparks and smoke. Today, fireworks are age-limited or banned in many towns. Back then, summer smelled like sulfur and freedom.

## #7 Swimming in the Creek

**s10 · photos**

On the hottest days, we didn't go to a pool. We went to the creek. A frayed rope hung from a big old tree, and you swung out, let go, and dropped into cold, brown water with a whoop. Mud squished between your toes. Your towel rode home draped over the bike handlebars. In some places, the older kids braved the quarry. There was no lifeguard and no whistle, just a buddy yelling, you okay? Today, quarries are fenced off, and signs line the banks. Back then, the creek was the best pool in town, and admission was free.

## #6 The Paper Route at Eleven

**s11 · photos**

At ten, eleven, twelve, plenty of us had a real job. The paper route. A canvas bag across your shoulder, so heavy it pulled you sideways on the bike. You rolled each paper tight, snapped on a rubber band, and aimed for the porch. Usually. Your fingers were black with ink. Friday, you knocked on doors to collect, and at Christmas the tips felt like a fortune. One Colorado man remembers subbing for a neighbor boy's route at eleven, for one dollar. Many were evening papers, perfect for after school. Today, it's adults in cars before dawn. Back then, an eleven-year-old ran a small business.

## #5 Forts in the Woods

**s12 · photos**

Every neighborhood had a wild place. A patch of woods, a vacant lot, a pile of dirt where a new house was going up. That's where we built our forts. Scrap boards, bent nails, and a hammer borrowed from Dad's workbench that never quite made it back. We had clubs with secret passwords and rules nobody remembered. We dug, we climbed, and we came home filthy and starving. No adult ever inspected the fort. Good thing too, because it leaned. Today, building sites are fenced, and open lots have filled in. Back then, the whole neighborhood was our playground.

## #4 Pond Hockey and Road Hockey

**s13 · photos**

When winter came and the pond froze solid, nobody waited for a schedule. We shoveled the ice ourselves, laced up skates with stiff fingers, and played until we couldn't feel our toes. Boots lined up on a log. A wooden stick taped at the blade. In Canada, and across the Upper Midwest and New England, it was a way of life. And when there was no ice, there was road hockey. Somebody yelled, Car! The nets dragged to the curb, the car crept past, and the game started right back up. Today, it's supervised rinks. Back then, the ice was ours till dark.

## #3 Biking All Over Town, No Helmet

**s14 · photos**

Our bikes were freedom on two wheels. Across town, out to the ball field, to a friend's house three neighborhoods over. We clothespinned baseball cards to the spokes so they roared like a motorcycle. In nineteen sixty-three the banana seat style arrived, with high handlebars and room for a friend on the back. And helmets? There weren't any for kids. The first successful hard-shell bike helmet didn't appear until nineteen seventy-five. American state helmet laws began in nineteen eighty-seven, and Ontario's law for riders under eighteen came in nineteen ninety-five. Back then, we just rode off with our hair full of wind.

## #2 Home When the Streetlights Came On

**s15 · photos**

We left after breakfast, and the only rule was simple. Be home when the streetlights come on. All day, nobody could reach us. No phone in our pocket, no way to check in. We drank from the garden hose, ate lunch at whatever house we happened to be near, and played until the sky turned orange. Then that first street lamp buzzed and flickered on, and mothers called names from porches up and down the block. Some rang a bell. Some just whistled. In a Canadian winter, the lights came on around half past four. Back then, the streetlight was our only clock.

## #1 Walking to School at Six

**s16 · photos**

And number one. We walked to school. Alone. At six years old. Rubber boots, a metal lunch pail, leaves crunching underfoot, crossing streets all by ourselves. Older kids walked the little ones, a sister holding her brother's hand. Canadian kids did it through snowbanks. In nineteen sixty-nine, almost half of American kids from kindergarten to eighth grade usually walked or biked to school. If you lived within a mile, it was eighty-nine percent. By two thousand nine, the overall number had dropped to just thirteen percent. Today, the drop-off line wraps around the block. Back then, the walk to school was our first taste of growing up.
