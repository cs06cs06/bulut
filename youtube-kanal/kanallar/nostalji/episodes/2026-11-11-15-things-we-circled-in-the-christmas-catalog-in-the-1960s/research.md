# Research: 15 Things We Circled in the Christmas Catalog in the 1960s

> **Note for the script writer:** My web search tool hit its usage limit partway through, so I could only check about half of these items. Every fact with a citation comes from a source I read. Anything marked **(unverified)** comes from general knowledge. Do not state it as fact until someone checks it. The verified items are the safest choices for the final 15. Toy names are trademarks, so in the script, describe the toys and don't make claims about the companies.

## Candidate items

1. **The catalog arrives (the framing item / #1 contender)** — A thick, heavy book in the mailbox in early fall. Kids lay on the living-room rug with a pen, circled toys, folded down page corners and fought with siblings over it. | Kids called all three seasonal catalogs "wish books" — Sears, Penney's and Ward's, and the Wish Books arriving by mail weeks before Halloween marked the unofficial start of the season. Sears made the "Wish Book" name official in 1968. In 1968, the Wish Book ran 605 pages: 225 pages of toys and 380 pages of gifts for adults. (krcu.org; mymerrychristmas.com) | Today: the main Sears catalog ended in 1993, and lists are now made online. | US: Sears, Penney's and Ward's competed. Canada: see #2. | Image: two kids on their stomachs on a shag rug in lamplight, an open catalog, a ballpoint pen, circled toys, a page corner folded down.

2. **Canada's catalog: Eaton's (and Simpsons-Sears)** — Rural and Prairie families shopped for Christmas by mail order. | The Eaton's catalogue was published from 1884 to 1976. People called it the "Homesteader's Bible," the "Family Bible," the "Want Book" or the "Wishing Book." Eaton's president Earle Orser announced that the Spring and Summer 1976 catalogue would be the last. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eaton's_catalogue; weeklyvoice.com). Simpsons-Sears ran its own Canadian catalogue starting in the early 1950s **(unverified)**. | Today: the catalogue ended in early 1976 and Eaton's later closed. | This is a strong Canada-only item. | Image: a farmhouse kitchen table in winter, a catalogue open beside an oil lamp or radio, snow on the windowsill.

3. **A toy oven that baked with light bulbs** — A small turquoise stove, tiny cake pans, and a cake about the size of a coaster that smelled like warm vanilla. | The 1963 Kenner original, a turquoise range with a carrying handle and pretend stovetop, sold for $15.95. Kenner used two 100-watt light bulbs instead of a heating element to lower the risk of burnt fingers. That was a huge price for a toy then, equal to more than $160 today. (smithsonianmag.com; fox2detroit.com) | Today: when incandescent bulbs were phased out, around 2011, the oven switched to a heating element **(unverified)**. | Image: a girl in a ponytail and an apron, Mom watching, a little cake coming out of the slot, a 1960s kitchen.

4. **The fashion doll and her outfits** — A doll in a black-and-white swimsuit, with tiny shoes that always got lost. The outfits were sold separately, which made them great for later birthdays. | The first one sold for $3 in 1959, and extra outfits cost between $1 and $5. She was first shown on March 9, 1959, at the American International Toy Fair in New York. (study.com; valuemystuff.com) | Today: the top auction price for an original doll is $27,450, which makes a good grandkids hook: "check the attic." | Image: a doll case open on a bedspread, little hangers, a girl holding up a tiny dress.

5. **The red drawing screen you shook to erase** — Two white knobs, silver powder, and staircase lines when you tried to draw a circle. | It went on sale in the US on July 12, 1960, for $2.99. It was the hot toy for Christmas 1960. More than 100 million have been sold since. (history.com; toytales.ca; medium.com) | Today: still sold, but it competes with tablets. | Image: a boy on the floor twisting the knobs with his tongue out, a decorated tree behind him.

6. **Die-cast hot rods (and the orange track)** — Tiny cars with fast wheels that raced down orange plastic track looped off the kitchen table. | The first sixteen cars released in 1968 sold for 59 cents each. Today they cost about $1.25, less than inflation would predict. (completeset.com; yahoo.com) | Older matchbox-size cars from Britain were sold before 1968 **(unverified)**. | Today: still cheap, but first editions are collectibles. | Image: two brothers on a linoleum floor, track clamped to a table edge, cars mid-loop.

7. **Spirograph** — Plastic gear wheels, colored ballpoint pens and pushpins on cardboard. The gear slipped and ruined the design. | Kenner brought it to the US market in 1966 and sold it as a creative children's toy. By 1977, more than 30 million had been sold. The "Toy of the Year" award in 1967 came from the British Association of Toy Retailers, so don't call it a US award. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirograph; nostalgiacentral.com) | Image: kitchen table, a stack of swirling designs, four colored pens, a child concentrating.

8. **Creepy Crawlers: baking bugs on a hot plate** — Metal molds, bottles of liquid "goop," an open electric heater, a strong plastic smell, and rubbery spiders. | Mattel introduced it in 1964, after the idea first appeared in its 1963 Vac-U-Maker set. In the 1970s, stricter child-safety and toy-safety rules made it hard to keep selling a toy that got extremely hot. (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thingmaker; toytales.ca) | Today: an exposed hot plate for kids is unthinkable. The US Child Protection and Toy Safety Act of 1969 and Canada's Hazardous Products Act of 1969 are the likely legal hooks **(both unverified)**. | Image: a boy at a basement workbench pulling a rubber bug from a metal mold with tongs, a warm bulb glow.

9. **Lite-Brite** — Colored pegs pushed through black paper made a glowing picture in a dark bedroom. Pegs got lost in the carpet. | Hasbro first made it in 1967, and it used a single 25-watt light bulb. (museumofplay.org; time.com) | Today: LED versions exist. | Image: a dark room, a child's face lit by the glowing grid, pegs scattered on the carpet.

10. **The buzzing doctor game** — Metal tweezers, a red nose that lit up, and a loud BZZZT that made everyone jump. | Milton Bradley introduced it in 1965. The student who designed the prototype in 1964 sold his rights for $500 and the promise of a job. (thehenryford.org; board-games.fandom.com) | Image: a family around a card table, Dad's hand shaking, kids laughing.

11. **Big tin-and-plastic playsets** — Western forts, castles and farms. A lithographed tin building, bags of tiny figures, and a toy box full of plastic soldiers and horses. | Marx playsets were popular with baby boomers and appeared in the Wish Book. (berkshireeagle.com). Prices and years are **unverified**. | Image: a fort set up under the Christmas tree, figures everywhere, a boy in pajamas lying down to aim.

12. **The 12-inch action soldier** — Moving joints, a scar on the cheek, uniforms sold separately. It was a "doll for boys," though nobody called it that. | Introduced by Hasbro in 1964 for around $4 **(year likely, price unverified)**. | Image: a boy kneeling in snow with a soldier figure on Boxing Day/Dec 26. *Keep the war theme light.*

13. **A doll that talked when you pulled the string** — A ring on the back, a scratchy little voice, and a few phrases chosen at random. | Mattel, 1960, about 11 phrases **(unverified)**; price about $18 **(unverified)**. | Image: a little girl pulling the string with her head tilted to listen.

14. **The banana-seat bike** — High-rise handlebars, a long sparkly seat, streamers and playing cards in the spokes. | US "wheelie bike" craze, starting about 1963 **(unverified)**. In Canada, CCM made bikes **(unverified)**. | Today: kids wear helmets, and Canadian provinces passed helmet laws later **(unverified, check by province)**. | Image: a new bike with a bow under the tree, a sun-filled living room, a kid still in pajamas.

15. **Electric train around the tree** — The smell of a hot transformer and ozone, a whistle, a tiny headlight in a dark room, Dad building the layout late on Christmas Eve. | Even the first 1933 Sears Christmas catalog included Lionel electric trains. (mymerrychristmas.com). Sales dropped in the 1960s as slot cars took over **(unverified)**. | Image: an oval track around the tree base, a father on his knees, a child's face at track level.

16. **Slot-car racing sets** — A figure-eight track, hand controllers, cars flying off the curve, an electric smell. | Craze in the early-to-mid 1960s, including HO-scale home sets **(unverified)**. | Image: two kids on the basement floor with controllers, focused, wood paneling behind.

17. **3-D picture viewer and reels** — Click the lever, hold it to the window, see cartoons or national parks in 3-D. | Widely sold in the 1960s; reels came in packs of three **(unverified)**. | Image: a child holding the viewer up to frosty window light.

18. **First transistor radio (for older kids)** — A leather case, a single earphone, a top-40 station under the covers at night. | Common teen gift in the 1960s as prices fell **(unverified)**. | Image: a teenager in bed with a radio by the ear, blue night light.

19. **The chain-reaction mousetrap game** — Build the crazy contraption: crank, boot, bucket, falling ball, cage drop. | Ideal, 1963 **(unverified)**. | Image: kids leaning over the board as the ball rolls.

20. **Boxing robots** — Red versus blue, thumb buttons, the head popping up with a "clack." | Marx, mid-1960s **(unverified)**. | Image: two siblings hammering buttons, Grandma laughing.

21. **The twist-yourself party game** — A plastic mat with colored dots, a spinner, the whole family tangled up on Christmas night. | Milton Bradley, 1966 **(unverified)**. | Image: cousins piled up on a living-room mat.

22. **Toy cap guns and cowboy outfits** — Red paper cap rolls, the smell of gunpowder, a fringed vest, a holster. | Very common in 1950s–60s catalogs; a multi-weapon toy gun came out around 1964 **(unverified)**. | Today: realistic toy guns are rare, with orange tips in the US **(unverified)**. *Sensitive topic: handle gently or skip.* | Image: a boy in a cowboy hat on the porch, a puff of smoke from the cap.

23. **Steel toy trucks** — Heavy pressed-steel dump trucks and bulldozers that lasted for decades and were played with in the sandbox until spring. | **Unverified** details. | Image: a yellow steel dump truck in the snow, a mitten on the wheel.

24. **Chemistry sets and microscopes** — Little glass bottles, an alcohol burner, smells in the basement. | Chemicals were far more powerful than today's sets **(unverified)**. | Image: a basement workbench, a test tube over a small flame, safety glasses nowhere in sight.

25. **The family board game of life's road** — A plastic spinner, little cars, and peg people. | Milton Bradley, 1960 **(unverified)**. | Image: a family around the dining table after Christmas dinner.

26. **The super-bouncy ball** — A small ball that bounced over the house; sold in stockings. | Mid-1960s, under $1 **(unverified)**. | Image: a ball mid-bounce in a driveway, a kid looking up.

27. **Winter gear (strongly Canadian)** — A toboggan, new skates, a hockey stick, a flying saucer sled. | Catalog pages full of winter sports gear **(unverified)**. | Image: a frozen backyard rink, Dad flooding it with a hose, kids in tuques.
