# 15 Toys We Circled in the 1960s Christmas Wish Book — Remember #1?

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

It hit the mailbox weeks before Halloween, heavy as a phone book. We grabbed a pen and circled everything. Fifteen toys we wanted most. And number one? It baked a real cake with two light bulbs, and you can probably still smell it.

## #15 The Electric Train Around the Tree

**s02 · photos**

Remember the smell? Warm oil, hot metal, and that sharp electric tang from the transformer. The train circled the tree all Christmas morning, its tiny headlight sweeping across the presents. Dad had built the layout late on Christmas Eve, on his knees, muttering at a track joint that just wouldn't connect. Trains were catalog royalty. Even the very first Sears Christmas catalog, back in nineteen thirty-three, had electric trains in it. We lay with our chins on the carpet, eye level with the engine as it came round the bend. Today, most trees stand alone. Back then, it had its own railroad.

## #14 The Red Screen You Shook Clean

**s03 · photos**

A red frame. Two white knobs. A gray screen full of silver powder. Turn the left knob, the line went sideways. Turn the right, it went up and down. Try a circle, and you got a staircase. It went on sale in the US in July of nineteen sixty, for two dollars and ninety-nine cents, and by that December it was the toy every kid wanted under the tree. Made a mistake? Flip it over and shake. Gone. Over a hundred million have sold since. Kids today erase with a fingertip on glass. We erased with both arms and a good, hard shake.

## #13 The Click-and-Look 3-D Viewer

**s04 · photos**

You dropped in a round paper disk, held it up to the window, and pressed the little lever. Click. Suddenly the Grand Canyon was right there, deep and real. Click. A cartoon animal leaped toward your nose. You went around and around that little ring of pictures until your thumb got tired. On Christmas afternoon, the whole family took turns at the frosty window. Let me see, let me see! No batteries, no screen, no charger. Just daylight and a click. Kids today wear headsets that cost a small fortune. We had a window and a lever, and honestly, it felt like magic.

## #12 The Spinning Gear Drawing Set

**s05 · photos**

Plastic rings with teeth around the edges. Smaller gears with little holes. A few pushpins, paper on cardboard, and ballpoint pens in red, blue, green and black. You set the pen in a hole and went around, slow and careful. Loops became flowers. Flowers became galaxies. Then, right near the end, the gear slipped. One jagged line across the whole thing. The set came to the US market in nineteen sixty-six, and by nineteen seventy-seven, more than thirty million had been sold. Half the refrigerators on our street had a swirl on them. And somewhere underneath, one with a slip.

## #11 The Tin Fort Playset

**s06 · photos**

It came in a big flat box that rattled when you shook it. Inside, a tin building printed with logs and windows, bright as a postcard. Then bags and bags of little plastic figures. Cowboys, horses, wagons, fences. Some families got the western fort. Others got a castle or a farm. These big playsets were a baby boomer favorite, and they had their own spot in the Wish Book. By noon, the fort stood under the tree and we were flat on our bellies, lining up horses on the rug. By spring, the vacuum had eaten half the horses. Worth every one.

## #10 The Doll That Talked Back

**s07 · photos**

She had a little plastic ring on her back, tied to a string. You pulled it all the way out, let go, and leaned in close. A tiny, scratchy voice said something sweet. Pull again, and she said something different. You never knew which one you'd get, and that was the fun. Little girls sat on the stairs on Christmas morning, pulling that string over and over, heads tilted to listen. Brothers pulled it too, when nobody was looking. Today every toy talks, sings and connects to the internet. Back then, a doll saying hello was pure wonder.

## #9 The Glowing Peg Picture Board

**s08 · photos**

Turn off the bedroom light. Flip the switch. Suddenly, the black paper glowed. Red, yellow, blue, green, one little peg at a time. It first came out in nineteen sixty-seven, and the whole glow came from one single twenty-five-watt light bulb inside the box. We made sailboats, clowns and Christmas trees, our faces lit up like we were sitting by a campfire. The downside? Pegs. Everywhere. In the carpet, in the couch, in Dad's slipper at six in the morning. You could still find them in the rug by Easter. A whole picture show, powered by one little bulb. If this brought back a Christmas morning or two, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the sixties together.

## #8 Tiny Hot Rods and Orange Track

**s09 · photos**

Sixteen tiny cars came out in nineteen sixty-eight, sleek and shiny, with wheels that rolled faster than anything we'd ever seen. Fifty-nine cents apiece. And that orange plastic track! We clamped it to the kitchen table and sent it swooping down to the linoleum, through a loop, and straight into the dog. Brothers argued over whose car was fastest. Mothers stepped on the track in the dark. Here's the funny part. Those little cars cost about a dollar and a quarter today, less than inflation says they should. And those very first ones? Collectors pay real money now.

## #7 Canada's Wishing Book Winter Pages

**s10 · photos**

In Canada, the big book had another name. The Eaton's catalogue. Folks called it the Wishing Book, the Want Book, even the Homesteader's Bible. Out on Prairie farms, Christmas often came by mail, chosen right at the kitchen table. And the winter pages were the ones that wore thin. New skates. A wooden toboggan. A hockey stick, waiting for fresh tape. Dad had been flooding the backyard rink for a week, and by Christmas afternoon we were out there in tuques, falling down and loving it. Eaton's printed its last catalogue in nineteen seventy-six. We still know those pages by heart.

## #6 The Banana-Seat Bike Under the Tree

**s11 · photos**

You came down the stairs and there it was, standing next to the tree with a big red bow on the handlebars. A long, sparkly banana seat. Handlebars so tall you felt like a motorcycle rider. Plastic streamers hanging from the grips. By New Year's, a playing card was clothes-pinned to the fork, clacking in the spokes like a motor. In a lot of towns, you couldn't ride it until the snow melted, so it lived in the living room, and we sat on it anyway. No helmet, of course. Nobody we knew even owned one.

## #5 The Buzzing Doctor Game

**s12 · photos**

A cartoon patient lay on the board with a red nose and little holes all over. Your job was simple. Lift out the tiny pieces with metal tweezers. Don't touch the sides. Then, bzzzt! The nose lit up, everybody jumped, and Grandma spilled her coffee. It came out in nineteen sixty-five. And here's the kicker. The student who dreamed it up sold his rights for five hundred dollars and the promise of a job. Christmas night, the whole family crowded around the card table, Dad's hand shaking like a leaf. Turns out the steadiest hands in the house belonged to the kids.

## #4 The Twelve-Inch Doll for Boys

**s13 · photos**

About twelve inches tall, with knees and elbows that actually bent, and a little scar on his cheek. The toy makers never said doll. Neither did we. But that's what he was, a doll for boys, and every boy on the block wanted one. His extra outfits were sold separately, of course, so there were whole catalog pages of tiny boots and jackets to circle. On Boxing Day, as our Canadian friends call it, he headed out to the snowbank for a big adventure and came back with frozen boots. Boys today have figures by the dozen. In the mid-sixties, one was a whole world.

## #3 Baking Rubber Bugs on a Hot Plate

**s14 · photos**

This one would never make it to a store shelf today. You got metal molds shaped like spiders and worms, little bottles of colored liquid goop, and an open electric hot plate. You poured the goop in, set the mold on the heater, and waited. The smell of hot plastic filled the whole basement. Then you peeled out a rubbery, wiggly bug and chased your sister with it. It came out in nineteen sixty-four. By the seventies, stricter toy safety rules made a toy that got that hot very hard to keep selling. Most of us have a tiny burn story. We'd still do it again.

## #2 The Fashion Doll and Her Wardrobe

**s15 · photos**

She came in a black-and-white striped swimsuit, with tiny high heels that vanished within a week. She was first shown at a big toy fair in New York in March of nineteen fifty-nine, and she cost three dollars. Then came the clever part. Her outfits were sold separately, a dollar here, five dollars there. So those catalog pages of tiny dresses got circled twice, once for Christmas and again for birthdays. We carried her everywhere in a little case with tiny hangers. And grandkids, listen up. One original sold at auction for twenty-seven thousand four hundred fifty dollars. Go check the attic.

## #1 The Oven That Baked With Light Bulbs

**s16 · photos**

And here it is. A little turquoise stove with a carrying handle and a pretend stovetop. It came out in nineteen sixty-three for fifteen dollars and ninety-five cents. That was a huge price for a toy, more than a hundred and sixty dollars today. And there was no heating element inside. It baked with two one-hundred-watt light bulbs, a clever way to keep little fingers from getting burned. You mixed the batter, slid the tiny pan into the slot, and waited with your nose pressed close. Out came a cake the size of a coaster. The whole kitchen smelled like warm vanilla. Mom got the first bite.
