# Research: Halloween in the 1960s (US & Canada)

*Notes for the script: Facts marked **(unverified)** are from memory or weak sources and should not be stated as certain. The search tool hit its limit partway through, so a few "today" comparisons (Canadian clock changes, age limits) could not be checked. The latest spending figures I could confirm are for 2025, not 2026. To keep clear of brand claims, the notes say "boxed-costume makers," "a major candy-corn maker" and so on. The company names appear only in the source list.*

## Candidate items

1. **Costumes made from the hall closet** — A bedsheet with eyeholes became a ghost. Dad's old jacket and a bindle stick made a hobo. A witch wore a dime-store cone hat, a borrowed skirt and green greasepaint that smeared onto pillowcases for days. A pirate had a foil-wrapped cardboard sword and an eyebrow-pencil mustache. | Homemade costumes were sturdier than store-bought ones but "depended on a parent's patience" (go2tutors). Costumes also tended to be homemade when treats were homemade (Tasting Table). | Today kids rarely wear a costume put together from household items. | Same in both countries. | **Image:** a kitchen in 1962, mother cutting eyeholes in a white sheet while a boy stands on a chair, linoleum floor, warm lamp light.

2. **The boxed dime-store costume** — A thin vacuum-formed plastic mask on an elastic band, plus a printed vinyl or rayon smock that tied at the neck, sold in a box with a cellophane window. | Cost about $1–$3 in the 1960s, or roughly $10–$30 in today's money (The Hustle, citing AP). Antique Trader and Estate Sale Finder put it as low as about $1.25. Sold at five-and-dimes, drugstores and department stores. A few makers produced most of them. | Today's costumes are pricier, multi-piece and full-color, and kids rarely wear full plastic face masks. | Canadian chain-store availability is **(unverified)**. | **Image:** a five-and-dime aisle with stacked costume boxes and plastic faces peeking through cellophane, a child pointing.

3. **The sweaty plastic mask** — The air inside smelled of plastic. Your breath fogged up inside the mask, the eyeholes were cut badly and the thin elastic string snapped or tangled in your hair. | First-hand accounts in Antique Trader and Little Village describe the forehead sweat, the condensation and the scratchy eyeholes. | Many parents now steer kids toward face paint for visibility **(unverified as a general rule)**. | — | **Image:** close-up of a child pushing a plastic mask up onto her forehead under a porch light, cheeks flushed.

4. **TV turned costumes into characters** — The neighborhood went from skeletons to whichever TV hero was big that year. | TV ownership rose from about 9% of US households in 1950 to about 90% in 1960. US costume sales were an estimated $10M in 1960, and a costume executive said "Television did it." One maker said overall costume sales roughly tripled in the following years. In 1966 the same maker claimed about 2 million costumes of a single caped TV superhero (The Hustle). | Licensed costumes still lead the list (NRF 2025). | Canadian kids watched many of the same US shows **(unverified)**. | **Image:** three kids in identical superhero smocks sitting cross-legged in front of a wood-cabinet TV set.

5. **Halloween was a children's night** — Grown-ups opened the door. They did not dress up themselves. | According to one major maker, adults accounted for only about 10% of costume sales in earlier decades. By 1989 they bought about half of all costumes, and that year costume and accessory revenue reached about $300M (The Hustle). Through the 1950s and early '60s, full-face masks were the main costume piece sold (Montrose School article). | Adult costume parties and costume contests are now mainstream. | — | **Image:** a father in a cardigan with a pipe-free smile, holding the door open for small ghosts.

6. **Pillowcase hauls** — A flowered pillowcase, a brown paper grocery sack or even a garbage bag, dragged home heavy. | Recalled in The Globe and Mail, Little Village, Popthomology and The Buzz. One family-history site notes that plastic pumpkin pails and bags of wrapped candy in photos usually point to the 1970s or later (Ancestral Findings). | Plastic pumpkin buckets, branded totes and glow-in-the-dark bags. | Both countries. | **Image:** a living-room rug with a pillowcase emptied out, candy sorted into piles, kids in pajamas.

7. **Out with the big kids, no parents** — An older sibling was in charge, and parents stayed home. | One 1970s memoir describes a big sister who ignored her little sister for three hours in the dark, with no parents around (Little Village). | Parents now often walk along or drive. Trunk-or-treat events and malls have replaced some door-to-door rounds (Becker Library, Washington University; The Hustle). | — | **Image:** a dusk sidewalk with four kids of different heights, the oldest holding a flashlight, no adults in the frame.

8. **Every house on the street, on foot** — You stopped by every single house you could reach, and the routes stretched blocks. | Popthomology's memoir describes visiting every house on foot. Neighborhoods were full of baby-boom kids **(unverified for head counts)**. | Kids now visit fewer homes, and some are driven house to house **(unverified)**. | Rural kids may have been driven between farms **(unverified)**. | **Image:** a long suburban block with porch lights glowing one after another and small figures in the distance.

9. **It got dark early because the clocks had already changed** — Trick-or-treating happened in real darkness. | The Uniform Time Act of 1966 standardized US daylight saving time to run from the last Sunday in April to the last Sunday in October, so Halloween always fell on standard time. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 moved the end to the first Sunday in November starting in 2007, and the extra hour of trick-or-treating daylight was part of the pitch. Candy makers had wanted this since the 1960s (Today I Found Out, Wikipedia: Uniform Time Act, Candy pumpkin, WebExhibits). | Kids now usually get daylight saving time's extra evening hour on Halloween. | Canadian provinces set their own rules, and most adopted the 2007 dates **(unverified)**. | **Image:** a blue-black sky at 6 p.m., jack-o'-lanterns glowing, kids' silhouettes against a streetlight.

10. **The porch light was the invitation** — Light on meant come up. Light off meant skip this house. | Porch lights became a signal that a home welcomed visitors in the 1950s–60s (Ancestral Findings). | Still used today, along with apps and teal pumpkins **(unverified)**. | Both countries. | **Image:** a single yellow porch bulb, a screen door, a carved pumpkin on the step.

11. **Popcorn balls from the neighbor lady** — Fist-sized popcorn balls, sticky with molasses and butter or corn syrup, wrapped in waxed paper or plastic wrap and tied with ribbon. Some had candy corn pressed in. | A popcorn ball recipe appears in an 1861 housekeeping encyclopedia. They were a fixture of 1950s Halloween parties and doorsteps (Tasting Table, Yahoo/Southern Living, a vintage-recipes blog). | They mostly vanished from trick-or-treat bags after the tampering scares (see #33–34). | Both countries. | **Image:** a tray of wrapped popcorn balls on a doily by the front door, an aproned hand offering one.

12. **Homemade cookies, cake, apples, coins and trinkets** — Treats came from the kitchen, not a bulk bag. | Early trick-or-treaters received homemade cookies, cake or popcorn balls, plus apples, coins and small trinkets. Packaged and homemade treats were mixed in the bag until the 1970s, when parents grew suspicious of homemade items (Smithsonian National Museum of American History). | Only factory-sealed candy is socially acceptable now. | Both countries. | **Image:** a frosted cookie with an orange jack-o'-lantern face being dropped into a paper sack.

13. **Being invited inside** — A neighbor waved you into the kitchen for a snack or a game. | Trick-or-treaters "might also have been invited in" for snacks and Halloween games (Smithsonian). | Unthinkable today. Kids stay on the porch. | — | **Image:** costumed kids crowded in a warm kitchen doorway, a cider jug on the table.

14. **Caramel and candy apples** — Shiny red candy apples or caramel apples on sticks, usually wrapped in waxed paper. | Caramel apples took off in the 1950s, and an automated caramel-apple machine was invented in 1960 (Smithsonian). | Homemade apples are no longer accepted at the door. | — | **Image:** a cookie sheet of caramel apples cooling on a windowsill, the October light behind them.

15. **Plain apples in the bag (and nobody wanted them)** — Heavy, bruised and taking up candy space. | By the '70s and '80s an apple in the bag was a letdown, and publicized pin and razor reports finished them off (The Globe and Mail). | Whole fruit has essentially disappeared from Halloween. | Prairie roots: see #20. | **Image:** a lone red apple at the bottom of a candy pile, a disappointed boy in a cowboy hat.

16. **Pennies and nickels** — Some houses handed out coins. | Coins were among early treats (Smithsonian). | Rare today. | Canadian kids later collected "loonies" (UNICEF Canada). | **Image:** a palm full of pennies over a pillowcase.

17. **Wrapped candy slowly took over** — Early 1960s bags held a mix of homemade and wrapped candy. | From the 1950s, candy companies chased Halloween and smaller individually wrapped pieces appeared. Mostly women handed out treats and liked that the pieces needed no preparation (Smithsonian; Ancestral Findings). By the 1980s, wrapped candy was the standard (Ancestral Findings; KNKX). | Now 100% wrapped. | — | **Image:** a cut-glass bowl with a few wrapped candies next to a plate of cookies, both by the door.

18. **Candy corn everywhere** — Waxy tricolor kernels. | In the 1950s candy corn was advertised as a year-round candy. One major maker still produces about 9 billion pieces a year (Smithsonian). | Now strictly seasonal and a running joke. | — | **Image:** a glass candy dish of candy corn on a lace tablecloth.

19. **Paper-wrapped molasses chews and penny candy** — Little twist-wrapped chews at the bottom of the bag. | The Globe and Mail mentions little paper-wrapped molasses candies as the least-wanted treat. Specific penny-candy prices for the 1960s are **(unverified)**. | Mostly gone, apart from niche candy shops. | Common in Canadian bags. | **Image:** a pile of orange-and-black twist-wrapped candies on a kitchen table.

20. **"Halloween apples!" on the Canadian Prairies** — Kids sang it in two-note chant form instead of "trick or treat." | Used in Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta from the early 1900s, with reports from the 1950s into the 1980s. A 1955 Globe and Mail piece described streets ringing with the chant. Edmonton kids in the 1930s mostly went to businesses, and apples were the usual reward. By 1987 some Edmonton kids had never heard the phrase (The Globe and Mail; CBC; City of Edmonton Archives). | Almost extinct now. | **Canada only.** Barely known in BC or Ontario. | **Image:** a snowy Winnipeg doorstep, bundled kids holding pillowcases up to a woman in a housecoat.

21. **"Shell out!" and "Charity!"** — Other Canadian door calls. | Kids shouted "Shell out!" and in Quebec "Charity, please!" or "Charité!" A 1960 Ottawa Citizen weekend magazine noted that Canadian streets heard "Hallowe'en apples!", "Trick or treat!" or "Charity!" depending on region (Barry Popik; The Globe and Mail). | "Trick or treat" is now standard. | **Canada only.** The apostrophe spelling "Hallowe'en" was common in Canadian print. | **Image:** a Montreal walk-up with outdoor stairs, kids climbing them in costume.

22. **"Trick or treat, smell my feet…"** — The rhyme every kid knew. | In print since at least 1964 (Barry Popik). | Still alive. Good for the comments section. | Both countries. | **Image:** two giggling kids chanting at a door.

23. **"Trick or treat" may be Canadian** — Fun fact. | Barry Popik cites Ontario in 1917. Edmonton Archives cites Blackie, Alberta in 1927. Global News cites the Lethbridge Herald on Nov. 4, 1927. The Globe and Mail says Lethbridge is not the origin. **Treat the origin as contested.** | — | Canada. | **Image:** a yellowed 1920s Prairie newspaper page.

24. **UNICEF orange box** — A small orange cardboard box handed out at school, rattling with coins, carried alongside the candy bag. | Started on Halloween 1950 in Philadelphia, with $17 collected. By the 1960s millions of US kids carried the boxes. UNICEF won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1965. Halloween was declared "UNICEF Day" in the US in 1967, and by 1969 about 3.5 million American kids took part (Wikipedia; Nonprofit News Feed). Classrooms competed for the biggest total (Popthomology). | Collection has largely moved to stores, online giving and coin machines (Scholastic; Wikipedia). | See #25. | **Image:** a little girl in a witch hat shaking an orange box at a door.

25. **UNICEF in Canada** — The same orange box was a fixture for decades. | Canadian families began mailing Halloween coins to UNICEF USA in 1952. The Canadian UNICEF Committee formed in 1955 and its first campaign raised $15,000. Canadians have since raised more than $90M. In 2006 the campaign was redesigned around school activities because of logistics and safety worries about door-to-door coin collecting (UNICEF Canada; trickortreatforunicef.ca). | Door-to-door boxes are mostly gone. | **Canada-specific.** | **Image:** an Alberta classroom, a teacher handing out flat orange boxes to kids at wooden desks.

26. **Mischief Night: soaping windows** — Bars of soap scribbled on car and house windows the night before Halloween. | Recalled from Pennsylvania in the late 1950s, early '60s and 1965. One writer describes October chores scrubbing soap streaks off the family windows (Empty Branches; Tom Morphet; Hoaxes.org). | Mostly died out, and now treated as vandalism. | Both countries. | **Image:** the morning after, a boy with a bucket and rag scrubbing a soap-streaked picture window.

27. **What the night was called depended on where you lived** — Mischief Night, Devil's Night, Gate Night, Goosey Night, Moving Night, Cabbage Night, Mat Night. | Gate Night in Winnipeg, Thunder Bay and parts of the Dakotas. Mat Night (stealing doormats) in English-speaking Quebec. Cabbage Night in rural Niagara Falls, Ontario in the 1950s–60s. Goosey Night in North Jersey. Devil's Night in Michigan and western NY/PA (Wikipedia: Mischief Night). | Detroit's Devil's Night turned into serious arson from the late 1960s to the 1990s, so be careful how this is framed. | Strong Canadian angle. | **Image:** kids tiptoeing off with a neighbor's doormat under a streetlight.

28. **Toilet-paper trees, dried corn and the black-thread "web"** — Pranks that left a mess but no real damage. | Toilet paper draped in trees. Dried feed corn thrown at windows and siding ("Tick-Tack Night" in eastern PA). Black thread strung across a dark sidewalk (Hoaxes.org; NJ 101.5; Tom Morphet). | Rare today, and now viewed as vandalism. | — | **Image:** a maple tree draped in white streamers of toilet paper at dawn.

29. **Old-fashioned "tricks" that really happened** — Outhouses tipped, gates lifted off their hinges, pumpkins rolled down the street. | Earlier pranks included removing fences and gates (the origin of "Gate Night") and moving outhouses (The Globe and Mail). A late-'50s rural PA account includes tipping outhouses and rolling pumpkins (Hoaxes.org). | "Trick" is now just a word. As one Alberta historian put it, the trick part is basically gone. | Both countries, especially rural areas. | **Image:** a farm lane with an outhouse leaning at a crazy angle and kids running off laughing.

30. **Beggars' Night and set hours** — Some towns trick-or-treated on a different night or set hours. | A few towns held "Beggars' Night" on October 30. Others set afternoon hours for safety (Ancestral Findings). Des Moines and its suburbs still trick-or-treat on Beggars' Night (Wikipedia). | Set municipal hours are common now **(unverified as a trend)**. | Some Canadian towns now have age and curfew bylaws (e.g., Bathurst, NB) **(unverified)**. | **Image:** a small-town street at dusk with a hand-lettered "Beggars' Night 6–8" sign in a store window.

31. **Party games: bobbing for apples and apple-peel fortunes** — A washtub of cold water, wet hair, apples on strings. | Bobbing for apples in water or hanging from strings was a long-standing party game. Fortune telling with apple seeds and peels appears in 1920s accounts. By the '70s and '80s, hardly anyone bobbed anymore (The Globe and Mail). | Rare now because of germ worries **(unverified as the reason)**. | Both countries. | **Image:** a basement party, a girl in a cat costume face-first in a galvanized tub, kids cheering.

32. **The razor-blade scare began** — Late in the decade, newspapers started warning parents about the "plump red apple" from the kindly neighbor. | Reports of sharp objects in treats became visible in the 1960s–70s. The New York Times carried a warning in 1970. The "Halloween sadist" story took hold in the early 1970s (Baltimore Sun; This Week in Public Health; Best & Horiuchi 1985). Utah's state Safety Council tried to ban trick-or-treating in the 1970s (Axios). Edmonton Archives dates the first (false) poisoned-candy reports to the 1960s. | Sociologist Joel Best has tracked reports back to 1958. He found no confirmed case of a child killed or seriously hurt by a contaminated treat from a stranger, and most reports turned out to be hoaxes (Atlas Obscura; Best's own site). | Parents check candy to this day. | Both countries. | **Image:** a 1970 newspaper on a kitchen table, a mother frowning at an apple.

33. **1982: the year Halloween changed** — After deadly pain-reliever capsule tampering near Chicago in late September 1982, homemade treats disappeared. | Vineland, NJ banned trick-or-treating, and some suburbs in OH, PA, IL and MA followed. Hospitals offered X-rays of candy, including nine Chicago-area hospitals in 1984. A 1988 report estimated hospitals spent up to $1.4M a year screening candy. Candy orders dropped in 1983 (Mental Floss; Chicago magazine; Axios; UPI). One 1982 Utah hospital scanned 30 bags and found nothing (Axios). | The X-ray ritual has faded, but the "inspect everything" habit stayed. | Canadian X-ray programs **(unverified)**. | **Image:** this one shows the contrast: a 1980s hospital radiology room, a nurse holding a pillowcase up to an X-ray screen.

34. **You ate candy on the walk home** — Nobody inspected anything first. | After the 1980s, parents were told to check every piece and throw out homemade treats (Shortform; KNKX). One writer recalls her mother inspecting "every fun-sized piece" (Grim Historian). | "Check the candy first" is now standard advice. | — | **Image:** a kid in a sheet-ghost costume unwrapping a candy under a streetlight, mouth full.

35. **Costumes that could catch fire** — Cotton sheets, crepe paper, rayon and jack-o'-lanterns lit with real candles. | The Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953 targeted dangerously flammable clothing. In 1967 Congress extended it to paper, plastic, foam and other materials used in clothing (CPSC). Edmonton Archives lists costumes catching fire among early Halloween safety problems. | Battery tea lights and flame-resistant labeling are common now **(unverified details)**. | Canadian rules **(unverified)**. | **Image:** a candle-lit pumpkin on a porch step with a child's long crepe-paper cape fluttering nearby. Keep it safe-looking.

36. **"Glow" costumes for headlights** — Early reflective trim on costumes. | One 1960s boxed-costume line used reflective material to be seen in car headlights (Shroud of Thoughts blog, which is a weaker source). A claim that child pedestrian deaths are about four times higher on Halloween comes only from a weather-gadget company's blog: **(unverified)**. | Reflective tape, glow sticks and flashlights are now standard advice. | — | **Image:** a car's headlights catching a child's sparkly smock on a dark curb.

37. **Jack-o'-lantern carved on newspaper at the kitchen table** — Dad with a paring knife, kids scooping slimy seeds, seeds roasted afterward. | A common memory **(unverified)**. Today about 46% of Americans plan to carve a pumpkin (NRF 2025). | Carving kits and LED lights. | Both countries. | **Image:** a newspaper-covered table, a pumpkin lid off, a child elbow-deep in pulp, the smell of roasting seeds suggested by a baking sheet.

38. **Modest decorations** — A carved pumpkin, maybe paper cutouts in the window. | Paper die-cut decorations **(unverified for specifics)**. Molded plastic yard figures appeared from the late 1960s (Estate Sale Finder). | Americans now spend about $4.2B on decorations (NRF 2025 via WRNJ). | — | **Image:** a front window with a paper black cat and a skeleton taped up, lamplight behind.

39. **A cheap night then, a $13 billion night now** — The contrast segment. | US Halloween spending was a record $13.1B in 2025, or about $114.45 per person, with costumes $4.3B and candy $3.9B (NRF; WRNJ). Compare an estimated $10M US costume market in 1960 (The Hustle/AP). **2026 figures not verified.** | — | Canadian totals **(unverified)**. | **Image:** split frame, a 1960s dime-store counter beside a modern big-box seasonal aisle.

40. **Witches and ghosts still top the list** — Some things never change. | In 2025 the top children's costumes included a comic-book superhero, princess, witch and ghost (NRF via WRNJ). | Continuity is a warm closing beat. | — | **Image:** a grandmother in a sheet-ghost photo from 1961 held next to a grandchild in a ghost costume today.

41. **Bundled up over the costume** — Snowsuits and parkas hid the costume in cold regions. | **(unverified)**, but a classic Canadian and northern-US memory. | Costume makers now sell larger sizes made to go over coats **(unverified)**. | Strong Canadian and Prairie angle. | **Image:** a Saskatchewan boy in a parka with a plastic mask on top, snow on the ground.

42. **Lost kids and the policeman's ride home** — Small kids sometimes got lost in the crowds. | Lost children and stolen treats were the most common complaints in old Edmonton coverage. A 1947 Edmonton newspaper reported a lost girl being taken to police headquarters (Edmonton Archives). This predates the 1960s, so use it only as color. | — | Canada. | **Image:** a kindly officer kneeling beside a crying little witch under a streetlamp.
