# Halloween in the 1960s: 15 Things That Would Never Happen Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Pillowcases of candy, dark streets, no grown-ups. Nobody ever checked. Here are fifteen sixties Halloween habits that would never fly today. And number one? It came from the neighbor lady's kitchen, every kid ate it walking home, and today almost no parent would allow one bite.

## #15 The Bedsheet Ghost From the Closet

**s02 · photos**

Remember the hall closet on Halloween afternoon? Mom pulled out an old white bedsheet, picked up the scissors, and cut two eyeholes. Just like that, you were a ghost. Dad's old jacket and a stick with a bandana made a hobo. A witch got a cone hat from the dime store and green greasepaint that ended up on every pillowcase in the house for days. A pirate had a cardboard sword wrapped in foil and an eyebrow-pencil mustache. It all depended on a parent's patience. Today, costumes come in a bag with a tag. Back then, they came from the linen closet. And the eyeholes never lined up.

## #14 The Box With the Cellophane Window

**s03 · photos**

Then there was the store-bought kind. A box with a cellophane window, and a plastic face staring out at you from the shelf at the five-and-dime. Inside, a thin printed smock that tied at the neck and a mask on a skinny elastic string. It cost about one to three dollars, roughly ten to thirty in today's money. Remember wearing it? The air smelled like plastic. Your breath fogged up inside. The eyeholes were in the wrong place, and the string snapped or tangled in your hair by the third house. Today, kids rarely wear full plastic face masks. We wore ours pushed up on our foreheads, sweaty and happy.

## #13 Everybody Was the Same TV Hero

**s04 · photos**

In nineteen fifty, only about nine percent of American homes had a television. By nineteen sixty, it was about ninety percent. And Halloween changed almost overnight. The skeletons gave way to whatever TV hero was big that year. One costume executive put it simply. Television did it. In nineteen sixty-six, one maker claimed it sold about two million costumes of a single caped TV superhero. So you'd ring a doorbell, and three of you would be standing there in the exact same smock. On both sides of the border, it seemed like every kid watched the same shows. Same cape, same mask, same giggle.

## #12 Grown-Ups Just Opened the Door

**s05 · photos**

Here's something the grandkids find hard to believe. Halloween was a children's night. Grown-ups didn't dress up. They opened the door. Dad in his cardigan, Mom with the candy bowl, smiling down at the little ghosts on the step. One big costume maker said adults made up only about ten percent of costume sales in those earlier decades. By nineteen eighty-nine, grown-ups were buying about half of them. Today there are office costume contests and adult parties all month long. Back then, if your father touched a mask, it was because he was fixing yours. The night belonged to us.

## #11 Real Darkness Before Supper Was Cold

**s06 · photos**

Trick-or-treating back then happened in real darkness. Here's why. After the Uniform Time Act of nineteen sixty-six, daylight saving time in the States ended on the last Sunday in October. So Halloween always came after the clocks went back. By six o'clock the sky was blue-black, and the jack-o'-lanterns did all the work. Candy makers wanted that extra hour of daylight since the sixties. They finally got it in two thousand seven, when the change moved into November. In Canada, each province set its own clock rules. But for most of us, it was flashlights, streetlights, and that wonderful shiver. Darkness was half the fun.

## #10 Halloween Apples on the Canadian Prairies

**s07 · photos**

Now, if you grew up on the Canadian Prairies, you might not have said trick or treat at all. In Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta, kids sang out Halloween apples, in a little two-note chant. Picture a frosty Winnipeg doorstep, kids bundled up, pillowcases held high. Other Canadian kids hollered Shell out. In Quebec, you might hear Charity, please. A nineteen fifty-five newspaper described Prairie streets ringing with that chant. By the late eighties, some Edmonton kids had never even heard it. Did you sing it? Tell us in the comments. It's nearly gone now.

## #9 Dragging Home the Pillowcase

**s08 · photos**

No plastic pumpkin buckets. Those mostly came later, in the seventies. We carried a pillowcase. Maybe one with faded flowers, borrowed right off your own bed. Some kids used a brown paper grocery sack, which always tore at the bottom just when it got good. By the end of the night, you were dragging that pillowcase down the sidewalk, heavy and lumpy, bumping against your knees. Then came the best part. Dumping it all out on the living-room rug and sorting it into piles. Trades were negotiated like a business deal. Today, kids carry little branded totes. We carried our bedding, and we filled it. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the fifties and sixties together.

## #8 The Little Orange Box That Rattled

**s09 · photos**

Rattle, rattle. Remember the sound? Along with the candy bag, a lot of us carried a small orange cardboard box from school, for UNICEF. It started on Halloween nineteen fifty in Philadelphia, when kids collected seventeen dollars. By nineteen sixty-nine, about three and a half million American kids were taking part. In Canada, the national UNICEF committee formed in nineteen fifty-five, and its first campaign raised fifteen thousand dollars. Classrooms competed for the biggest total. Neighbors dropped pennies and nickels through the slot. Today, the door-to-door boxes are mostly gone. But the sound of those coins? You never forget it.

## #7 Every House, Every Porch Light

**s10 · photos**

Back then, you didn't pick and choose. You went to every single house you could reach. On foot. Blocks and blocks of it. The rule was simple. Porch light on meant come on up. Porch light off meant skip it, they're out, or they ran out. From the end of the street, you could see the lights glowing one after another, like a runway for little ghosts. Your legs ached. Your bag got heavy. You kept going anyway. These days, many families do a shorter loop, or a trunk-or-treat in a parking lot. We walked until our feet gave out.

## #6 Bobbing for Apples in the Washtub

**s11 · photos**

At the school or basement Halloween party, there was always the washtub. Galvanized metal, full of cold water, apples bobbing on top. Hands behind your back. Face in. Somebody always soaked their whole head and came up sputtering while everyone cheered. Sometimes the apples hung from strings, swinging just out of reach. And a few kids tried the old trick of peeling an apple in one long ribbon to tell a fortune. By the seventies and eighties, hardly anyone bobbed anymore. Everybody's face in the same tub of water? Today that would end the party. Back then, it was the party.

## #5 Soap on the Windows

**s12 · photos**

The night before Halloween had its own name, and it depended on where you lived. Mischief Night in Pennsylvania. Goosey Night in North Jersey. Gate Night in Winnipeg and Thunder Bay. Cabbage Night around rural Niagara. Mat Night in English-speaking Quebec, where doormats mysteriously wandered off. The classic trick was a bar of soap scribbled across a car window or a picture window. Toilet paper draped in the maple tree. Then the next morning came the bucket and the rag. Today, that's called vandalism, and it's mostly died out. Back then, it was just one more chore.

## #4 Come On In, Have Some Cider

**s13 · photos**

Some neighbors didn't just hand you something at the door. They waved you inside. Into a warm kitchen that smelled like cinnamon and coffee. A jug of cider on the table. Maybe a game, maybe a cookie, maybe the lady of the house guessing which one under the sheet was you. The Smithsonian notes that trick-or-treaters back then might be invited in for snacks and Halloween games. Nobody thought twice. Your mother knew that lady. That lady knew your mother. Today, kids stay on the porch, and that's that. But for a few minutes, the whole street was one big family kitchen.

## #3 Out With the Big Kids, No Parents

**s14 · photos**

Here's the one that makes young parents gasp. We went out without any grown-ups at all. An older brother or sister was in charge, maybe twelve years old, holding a flashlight. Mom and Dad stayed home handing out candy. And that big sister? Half the time she ditched you to walk with her friends, and you just tagged along behind in the dark. For two or three hours. Nobody carried a phone, because there wasn't one to carry. You came home when the bag was full or your feet gave out. Today, parents walk along or drive. Back then, the big kids were the grown-ups.

## #2 Nobody Ever Checked the Candy

**s15 · photos**

And the candy itself? You started eating it on the sidewalk. Under the streetlight, through the eyeholes of your sheet, before you reached the next house. Nobody inspected a thing. Then, around the end of the sixties and into the seventies, newspapers started warning about the plump red apple from the kindly neighbor. In nineteen eighty-two, after a terrible tampering case near Chicago, some hospitals even offered to X-ray candy. Here's the twist. A sociologist who traced these stories back to nineteen fifty-eight found no confirmed case of a child killed or seriously hurt by a stranger's Halloween treat. Most were hoaxes. We still check every piece. Back then, nobody ever checked.

## #1 The Neighbor Lady's Popcorn Balls

**s16 · photos**

And number one. You know the one. The neighbor lady's popcorn balls. Fist-sized, sticky with butter and molasses or corn syrup, wrapped in waxed paper and tied with a bit of ribbon. Some had candy corn pressed right in. A recipe for them shows up in a housekeeping book from eighteen sixty-one. Beside them, homemade cookies with jack-o'-lantern faces, and caramel apples cooling on her windowsill. Every kid on the block ate one on the walk home, fingers glued together. By the seventies, parents grew suspicious of anything homemade, and those treats vanished. Today, almost no parent would let you take a single bite. Oh, but they were good.
