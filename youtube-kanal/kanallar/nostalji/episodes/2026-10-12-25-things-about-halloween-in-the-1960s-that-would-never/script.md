# 25 Things About Halloween in the 1960s That Would Never Happen Today

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Remember the sound of a brown paper sack tearing at the bottom, halfway down the block, and the panic of catching your candy before it hit the sidewalk? Remember the smell of a plastic mask, warm with your own breath, and the crunch of dry leaves under your sneakers in the dark? That was Halloween when we were kids. Nobody drove us door to door. Nobody checked our candy before we ate it. Our costumes came out of the hall closet or a thin cardboard box from the five-and-dime, and the neighbor lady down the street had been making popcorn balls since lunchtime. It was a night that belonged to children, and for a few hours the whole neighborhood seemed to belong to us too. So tonight, let's walk those streets again together. I've gathered twenty-five things about Halloween in the sixties that would never happen today, from pillowcase hauls to soaped windows to a little orange box that rattled with pennies. Some will make you laugh, and a few might make you a little misty. Before we start, tell me in the comments where you grew up, and what you dressed up as the year you remember best.

## 1. Costumes Made From the Hall Closet

**s02 · photos**

Let's start where most of us started: with a bedsheet. In a lot of homes, a Halloween costume didn't come from a store at all. It came from the hall closet, the attic trunk, or Dad's side of the wardrobe. An old white sheet, two eyeholes cut with Mom's sewing scissors, and you were a ghost. Dad's worn-out jacket and a stick with a bandana bundle tied on the end, and you were a hobo. A witch needed a cone hat from the dime store, a borrowed black skirt and green greasepaint that somehow ended up smeared on the pillowcases for days. Pirates carried cardboard swords wrapped in kitchen foil and wore mustaches drawn on with an eyebrow pencil. These costumes were sturdier than anything from a box, and they lasted about as long as a parent's patience did. They were normal because money was careful and imagination was free. Today, very few children walk out the door in something pieced together from the linen closet. But I'll bet you can still feel that sheet slipping over one eye as you tried to find the next porch step in the dark.

## 2. Dragging Home a Heavy Pillowcase

**s03 · photos**

Then came the bag. Not a plastic pumpkin with a handle, not a glow-in-the-dark tote. We grabbed a pillowcase off the bed, maybe one with faded flowers on it, or a brown paper grocery sack from under the kitchen sink. Some kids, the really ambitious ones, brought a garbage bag. The paper sacks were a gamble. By the twentieth house the bottom went soft, and if there had been any rain, you could lose your whole night's work in one terrible rip. A pillowcase was the professional's choice. It got heavier and heavier, until you were hauling it over your shoulder like Santa Claus in reverse, the corners bulging with apples and popcorn balls. Nobody thought twice about it. You simply used what was in the house. Family historians say that if you spot plastic pumpkin pails in an old Halloween photograph, it probably dates from the seventies or later. Today, the pillowcase has mostly retired from the job. And Mom, I'm sure, was relieved not to scrub chocolate and green greasepaint out of the good linens every November.

## 3. Out With the Big Kids, No Parents

**s04 · photos**

Here's one that makes younger parents gasp. When we went trick-or-treating, our parents usually didn't come along. They stayed home, handed out treats, and sent us off with an older brother or sister in charge. In theory, the big kid was looking after us. In practice, the big kid walked ten steps ahead with friends and pretended not to know us. One woman remembered her big sister ignoring her for three straight hours in the dark, with not a grown-up in sight. And she turned out just fine. The rules were simple: stay together, mind your brother, and be home by a certain time. The streets were full of other children, the neighbors knew every family, and nobody considered it reckless. It was just Halloween. Today, many parents walk right alongside their kids or drive them from house to house, and plenty of families go to trunk-or-treat events in school parking lots or to the mall instead. Times changed for their own reasons. But oh, that first taste of freedom, out in the dark with a flashlight and a big sister who wished you'd go home.

## 4. The Boxed Costume From the Five-and-Dime

**s05 · photos**

If your family did buy a costume, it came in a box. Remember those? A thin cardboard box with a cellophane window, and through it, a plastic face staring back at you from the shelf at the five-and-dime, the drugstore or the department store. Inside was a molded mask on a skinny elastic band and a printed smock, usually vinyl or rayon, that tied at the back of the neck. The picture printed on the smock told everyone who you were supposed to be, which was helpful, because the smock itself was shaped like a sack. These costumes cost about one to three dollars back then, which works out to roughly ten to thirty dollars in today's money. Some sold for as little as a dollar and a quarter. In nineteen sixty, Americans spent an estimated ten million dollars on costumes altogether. In twenty twenty-five, they spent around four point three billion. Today's costumes come in pieces, in full color, with gloves and wigs and capes. Ours crinkled when we walked and ripped by the second Halloween. And we loved them anyway.

## 5. The Sweaty Plastic Mask

**s06 · photos**

Now let's talk about actually wearing that mask. The moment you snapped it on, the whole world shrank down to two little eyeholes that never quite lined up with your real eyes. The air inside smelled like a brand-new shower curtain. Within a block, your own breath fogged up the plastic, and a little line of sweat formed right across your forehead. The edges of the eyeholes scratched, the nose holes were too small, and that thin elastic string either snapped at the third house or wound itself so tightly into your hair that Mom had to cut it out. So what did every one of us do? We pushed the mask up on top of our head between houses, cheeks red and damp in the cold air, and pulled it back down just before we knocked. Those masks were normal because they were cheap and they looked just like the picture on the box. Today, a lot of parents choose face paint instead, so kids can see where they're going. Sensible, I suppose. But nothing in this world smells quite like the inside of a Halloween mask from the sixties.

## 6. When Television Picked Our Costumes

**s07 · photos**

Something new happened to Halloween in our childhood, and it came through the living room. In nineteen fifty, only about nine percent of American households had a television. By nineteen sixty, it was about ninety percent. Suddenly, the neighborhood wasn't just ghosts and skeletons anymore. It was whoever was big on television that year: cowboys, space heroes, cartoon characters and caped crusaders. One costume executive summed it up in three words: Television did it. One maker said its costume sales roughly tripled in the years that followed, and in nineteen sixty-six it claimed to have sold about two million costumes of a single caped TV superhero. Imagine that. You'd ring a doorbell and find three other kids on the porch wearing exactly the same smock as you. Canadian kids watched many of the same programs, so I suspect porches up north looked rather familiar too. Today, costumes based on movies and shows still top the list. So in a way, we started it. We were the first generation to dress up as whatever we saw flickering on that glowing screen.

## 7. Halloween Belonged to the Kids

**s08 · photos**

Back then, Halloween was a children's night, plain and simple. The grown-ups had one job, and it was opening the door. Dad answered in his cardigan and slippers, Mom stood behind him with the treat bowl, and maybe somebody's grandpa pretended to be terribly frightened by a four-year-old in a sheet. Adults generally did not dress up. According to one major costume maker, grown-ups made up only about ten percent of costume sales in those earlier decades. By nineteen eighty-nine, adults were buying about half of all the costumes sold. Today, there are office costume contests, grown-up parties, and parents who coordinate their outfits with the whole family. Some neighbors put more work into their own costume than the children do into theirs. There's nothing wrong with that, of course. Everyone deserves a little fun. But there was something special about a night when the whole world was arranged just for us. The adults stood in the warm, lit doorways and played the audience. And we, in our crooked masks and dragging sheets, were the show.

## 8. Every House on the Street, on Foot

**s09 · photos**

Our routes were serious business. We didn't skip houses, and we certainly didn't ride in the car. We walked to every single door we could reach, up one side of the street and down the other, then around the corner and on to the next block. Some of us covered what felt like miles, though our short legs may have exaggerated a bit. And we all knew the rules of the road. A porch light on meant come on up, we're expecting you. A porch light off meant this house is sitting it out, so don't bother knocking. You'd stand at the end of the block and look down a long row of yellow bulbs glowing one after another, and it felt like the whole street was waving you in. Out in the country, things were different, and farm kids might have been driven from one place to the next. Today, many children visit far fewer homes. But I can still feel that tired, happy ache in my legs at the end of the night. If you remember mapping out your route, tell me in the comments which street in your town gave the best treats.

## 9. Trick-or-Treating in Real Darkness

**s10 · photos**

Here's a detail most of us never thought about as kids. When we went trick-or-treating, it was truly dark. Not dusky, not twilight. Dark. That's because the clocks had already fallen back. In the United States, the Uniform Time Act of nineteen sixty-six set daylight saving time to end on the last Sunday in October, which meant Halloween always landed on standard time. By suppertime the sky was blue-black, and the jack-o'-lanterns glowed like little orange lamps along every porch. The streetlights hummed, the leaves skittered across the pavement, and every shadow behind a hedge was a possible monster. It made the whole night feel mysterious. Then, starting in two thousand seven, the American end of daylight saving moved to the first Sunday in November, and part of the argument was giving kids an extra hour of daylight for trick-or-treating. Candy makers had wanted that change since the sixties. In Canada, each province sets its own rules about the clocks. An extra hour of light may be safer. But we walked through a world of real darkness, lit by candles and porch bulbs, and it was magic.

## 10. Popcorn Balls From the Neighbor Lady

**s11 · photos**

Every street had one. The neighbor lady who started cooking right after lunch and had her kitchen windows steamed up by three o'clock. When you knocked on her door, she held out a tray of popcorn balls, each one about the size of your fist, sticky with butter and molasses or corn syrup. They were wrapped in waxed paper or plastic wrap, sometimes tied with a little ribbon, and if she was feeling generous, there were candy corns pressed right into the sides. You could feel the stickiness right through the wrapper. People had been making popcorn balls for a very long time. A recipe shows up in a housekeeping encyclopedia from eighteen sixty-one, and by the fifties they were a fixture at Halloween parties and on doorsteps all across the continent. Today, you'll almost never find a homemade popcorn ball in a trick-or-treat bag. After the tampering scares of later years, they quietly disappeared. But somewhere in your memory, there's still a kind woman in an apron, smiling down at you over a tray of golden popcorn and saying, go ahead, dear, take two.

## 11. Cookies, Pennies and Little Trinkets

**s12 · photos**

Our bags were a mixed treasure chest. Along with some store-bought candy, we got whatever came out of the neighbors' kitchens and pockets. Frosted sugar cookies with orange jack-o'-lantern faces. Squares of cake wrapped in a napkin. Little trinkets, a tiny toy or a trinket from the dime store. And at certain houses, coins. A couple of shiny pennies or a nickel dropped into your sack with a clink, and later you'd dig for them like buried treasure. Historians at the Smithsonian say that early trick-or-treaters received exactly this kind of mix: homemade cookies, cake, popcorn balls, apples, coins and small trinkets. Homemade and packaged treats sat side by side in the bag until the seventies, when parents grew suspicious of anything that wasn't sealed in a factory. Today, a frosted cookie handed out at the door would be thrown away before it ever reached a child's mouth. That's a little sad, really. Those cookies were baked by someone who knew your name, knew your mother, and had probably watched you learn to ride your bike right in front of her house.

## 12. Being Invited Inside for Cider

**s13 · photos**

Now here's one that would truly never happen today. Sometimes, a neighbor didn't just hand you a treat at the door. She waved you right inside. Come in, come in, it's cold out there. And in you'd go, a little crowd of ghosts and pirates, dropping leaves all over her clean kitchen floor. There might be a jug of cider on the table, a plate of doughnuts, or a game set up in the den. The house smelled of cinnamon and warm radiators, and you'd stand there with your mask pushed up while she tried to guess who was hiding under each costume. The Smithsonian notes that trick-or-treaters in those days might well be invited in for snacks and Halloween games. It was normal because neighborhoods were small worlds. Everyone knew everyone, and a child at the door was somebody's child, which meant, in a way, everyone's child. Today, children stay on the porch, and their parents would be alarmed if they didn't. I understand why. But I miss being welcomed into a warm kitchen on a cold October night, just for showing up.

## 13. Caramel Apples and Candy Apples

**s14 · photos**

Some houses went all out with apples. Not plain ones, but shiny red candy apples with a hard, glassy shell, or caramel apples on wooden sticks, cooling on a cookie sheet by the kitchen window. They came wrapped in waxed paper, and getting one was a very big deal. Biting into a candy apple took real courage. The shell cracked like ice on a puddle, stuck to your teeth, and left a red ring around your mouth for the rest of the night. Caramel apples were softer and chewier, and they somehow always ended up glued to the inside of your pillowcase. Caramel apples really took off in the fifties, and in nineteen sixty someone even invented an automated machine to make them. But the best ones were still made at home, by somebody standing at the stove stirring a pot for the better part of an hour. Today, no parent would let a child eat a homemade candy apple from a neighbor's door. Still, close your eyes and you can probably hear that crack of the candy shell, and taste the cold, sweet apple underneath.

## 14. The Treats Nobody Wanted

**s15 · photos**

Let's be honest. Not every treat was a winner. There was always one house that handed out plain apples. Big, heavy, ordinary apples that thumped into your bag and took up all the room where candy should have gone. By the end of the night they were bruised from bumping against everything else. And then there were those little paper-wrapped molasses chews, twisted at both ends, in orange and black. They seemed to multiply at the bottom of the bag. Kids traded them, hid them, or left them sitting in the candy dish until Christmas. By the seventies and eighties, an apple in your bag was a genuine letdown, and the scare stories of those years finished off fruit at Halloween for good. Today, whole fruit has all but vanished from trick-or-treating, and those molasses chews have mostly retreated to old-fashioned candy shops. And yet here's a funny thing. Ask anyone our age to describe their Halloween bag, and those chews come up first. The treats we didn't want turned out to be the ones we remember best.

## 15. Halloween Apples on the Prairies

**s16 · photos**

Speaking of apples, here's a tradition our Canadian friends on the Prairies will know. In Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta, a lot of kids didn't say trick or treat at all. They sang out Halloween apples, in a sing-song, two-note chant, standing on the doorstep with their pillowcases held open. The custom goes back to the early nineteen hundreds, and people remember it from the fifties right into the eighties. In nineteen fifty-five, a national newspaper described Prairie streets ringing with that chant. Back in the thirties, Edmonton kids mostly went around to local businesses, and an apple was the usual reward, which helps explain the name. Picture it: a cold Winnipeg evening, maybe the first snow already on the ground, and a neighbor in her housecoat opening the door to a chorus of little voices. By nineteen eighty-seven, some children in Edmonton had never even heard the phrase. Today it's almost gone, and it was never well known in British Columbia or Ontario. If you grew up singing it, you carry a little piece of Prairie history that most of the continent never heard.

## 16. Shell Out, Charité and Smelly Feet

**s17 · photos**

Elsewhere in Canada, the words at the door varied too. In some places kids hollered, shell out! In Quebec, you might hear charité, or charity, please. A Canadian weekend magazine in nineteen sixty noted that depending on where you lived, the streets rang with Hallowe'en apples, trick or treat, or charity. And yes, Canadian newspapers often spelled it with that little apostrophe in the middle, Hallowe'en. Interestingly, some of the earliest printed uses of the phrase trick or treat turn up in Canada, in Ontario in nineteen seventeen and in Alberta in nineteen twenty-seven, though where it truly began is still debated. And then there was the rhyme every kid on both sides of the border seemed to know: trick or treat, smell my feet, give me something good to eat. It's been found in print since at least nineteen sixty-four, and children are still giggling it on doorsteps today. Some things survive everything. Tell me in the comments what you shouted at the door where you grew up, and whether you finished that rhyme the polite way or the other way.

## 17. The Little Orange UNICEF Box

**s18 · photos**

Along with the pillowcase, many of us carried something else: a small orange cardboard box, handed out at school, with a slot in the top for coins. Trick-or-treat for UNICEF began on Halloween in nineteen fifty in Philadelphia, when children collected seventeen dollars. By the sixties, millions of kids carried those boxes. UNICEF won the Nobel Peace Prize in nineteen sixty-five, Halloween was declared UNICEF Day in the United States in nineteen sixty-seven, and by nineteen sixty-nine about three and a half million American children took part. Classrooms competed to see who could bring back the most. In Canada, families started sending their Halloween coins in nineteen fifty-two, the Canadian UNICEF committee formed in nineteen fifty-five, and its first campaign raised fifteen thousand dollars. Canadians have given more than ninety million since. You remember the sound, don't you? That rattle of pennies and nickels as you held it up at the door. Today, the giving has mostly moved into schools, stores and online. But for many of us, that little orange box was the first time we understood that our Halloween could help a child far away.

## 18. Bobbing for Apples in a Washtub

**s19 · photos**

At Halloween parties, in basements and school gyms and community halls, there was always a galvanized washtub full of cold water and floating apples. You knelt down, hands behind your back, and plunged your face in while everybody cheered. The apples bobbed away from your teeth like they had minds of their own. You came up gasping, hair dripping, costume soaked down the front, and if you finally caught one, you held it in your teeth like a trophy. Some parties hung apples from strings instead, which was even harder. And the older girls might peel an apple in one long curl and toss it over a shoulder to see what letter it made on the floor, an old fortune-telling game that goes back generations. By the seventies and eighties, hardly anyone bobbed for apples anymore. Today, the idea of twenty children sharing one tub of water would send most parents reaching for the hand sanitizer. But oh, the shrieking and the laughter, and the cold water running down your neck. Nobody ever went home dry from a Halloween party.

## 19. Mischief Night and Soaped Windows

**s20 · photos**

For some kids, the fun started the night before. In many places, October thirtieth was Mischief Night, and the classic prank was soaping windows. A bar of soap borrowed from the bathroom, a car parked at the curb, and a few squiggly streaks across the windshield. Picture windows got the same treatment. What that night was called depended entirely on where you lived. In Winnipeg, Thunder Bay and parts of the Dakotas, it was Gate Night. In English-speaking Quebec, it was Mat Night, when doormats mysteriously wandered off. In rural parts of the Niagara Falls area of Ontario, folks called it Cabbage Night, and in North Jersey, it was Goosey Night. And here's the part we don't like to admit: the next morning, somebody had to scrub that soap off. One writer remembered October chores with a bucket and rag, cleaning soap streaks off her own family's windows. Today, Mischief Night has mostly faded away, and soaping a car would be treated as vandalism. Back then it was a cheeky rite of passage, and most of us learned that soap is a lot harder to wash off than to put on.

## 20. Toilet Paper Trees and Tipped Outhouses

**s21 · photos**

Some pranks went a little further. On certain mornings after Halloween, you'd wake up to find the big maple tree in the front yard draped in long white streamers of toilet paper, fluttering like ribbons in the breeze. In eastern Pennsylvania, kids threw handfuls of dried corn against windows and siding, and some strung black thread across a dark sidewalk just to startle whoever walked into it. Out in the country, the old tricks were bolder still. Gates were lifted right off their hinges, which is where the name Gate Night came from. Pumpkins went rolling down the lane. And yes, more than one outhouse ended up tipped over on its side. Most of it left a mess but no real harm, and the grown-ups grumbled and cleaned up. Today, the trick part of trick or treat is pretty much gone. As one Alberta historian put it, the trick part has basically disappeared. Children say the words without meaning a thing by them. And maybe that's for the best. But somebody's grandpa, somewhere, is still chuckling about that outhouse.

## 21. Crepe Paper Capes and Candlelit Pumpkins

**s22 · photos**

Looking back, some of our Halloween gear was a little worrying. Costumes were made of cotton sheets, crepe paper, rayon and whatever else was lying around the house. Witches trailed long capes. Ghosts dragged their sheets along the ground. And every porch step had a jack-o'-lantern lit with a real candle, flickering right at the height of a swishing hemline. Grown-ups reminded us to stay away from the pumpkins, and mostly we did. Still, costumes catching fire were among the early Halloween safety problems that city officials worried about. In the United States, the Flammable Fabrics Act of nineteen fifty-three went after dangerously flammable clothing, and in nineteen sixty-seven Congress widened it to cover paper, plastic, foam and other materials used in clothing. These days you'll see plenty of battery-powered lights glowing inside pumpkins, and costume safety gets talked about far more than it did then. That's one change we can all be glad about. But I still love the memory of a real candle glowing in a pumpkin's grin, and the smell of a pumpkin lid scorched just a little on top.

## 22. A Parka Over the Costume

**s23 · photos**

For those of us who grew up in the colder parts of the continent, there's a special Halloween memory, and it wasn't a happy one at the time. You spent weeks planning your costume. You finally had the perfect witch dress or cowboy vest. And then, right at the front door, Mom held out the snowsuit. A lot of us remember trick-or-treating in a parka, with the costume squashed underneath and only the plastic mask perched on top to show who we were supposed to be. On the Prairies, and across much of Canada and the northern states, a snowy Halloween was nothing unusual. You'd see a skeleton in mittens, a princess in snow boots, a pirate with a toque pulled down over his eye patch. Nobody could tell what anybody was, and nobody really cared. Halloween was cold, and mothers won that argument every single time. Today's kids in warmer towns may never know the feeling. But looking back, it wasn't a costume ruined. It was a mother making sure her child came home warm.

## 23. Carving the Pumpkin on Newspaper

**s24 · photos**

A few days before the big night, the kitchen table disappeared under yesterday's newspaper. The pumpkin sat in the middle like a guest of honor. Dad, or sometimes Mom, took a paring knife and cut a lid around the stem, and then it was the kids' turn. We plunged our hands into the cold, slimy insides and pulled out strings and seeds by the fistful, squealing about how awful it felt. Then came the face: triangle eyes, a triangle nose, and a grin with a crooked tooth or two. Afterward, the seeds went onto a baking sheet with a little salt, and the house smelled toasty for the rest of the evening. That pumpkin was often most of the decorating. Maybe a paper black cat or a cardboard skeleton taped up in the front window, and that was it. Today, about forty-six percent of Americans still plan to carve a pumpkin, but there are kits, stencils and little electric lights, and Americans spend about four point two billion dollars a year on Halloween decorations. Ours cost the price of a pumpkin and a few sheets of newspaper. And it was plenty.

## 24. Eating Candy on the Walk Home

**s25 · photos**

And here's the big one. On the walk home, we ate our candy. Right there on the sidewalk, under the streetlights, mask pushed up, mouth full. Nobody inspected anything first. Popcorn balls, cookies, an unwrapped caramel, it all went straight down the hatch. Toward the end of the sixties, though, newspapers began warning parents about poisoned treats and razor blades hidden in apples, and by the early seventies, the idea of a stranger tampering with Halloween candy had firmly taken hold. Then, in nineteen eighty-two, deadly tampering with pain-reliever capsules near Chicago frightened the whole continent, and homemade treats vanished almost overnight. Some hospitals even offered to X-ray children's candy. Here's what's worth knowing, though. A sociologist named Joel Best has studied these reports going all the way back to nineteen fifty-eight, and he found no confirmed case of a child killed or seriously hurt by a contaminated treat from a stranger. Most stories turned out to be hoaxes. Still, check the candy first became the rule, and it's the rule today. Tell me in the comments: did your parents ever check your candy, or was half of it gone before you got home?

## 25. Little Ghosts Still Knock on Doors

**s26 · photos**

Let's end with something that hasn't changed at all. In twenty twenty-five, when American families were asked about their children's costumes, two of the most popular choices were still a witch and a ghost. After all the plastic masks and superhero smocks, after the snowsuits and the scares and the billion-dollar store aisles, a child with a white sheet over her head and two eyeholes cut out is still knocking on doors. Somewhere tonight, maybe, a grandmother is opening an old photo album to a faded snapshot from nineteen sixty-one: a little ghost on the front steps, sheet dragging on the porch, pillowcase in hand. And beside her, a grandchild is holding up a ghost costume of her own and asking, Grandma, is that you? The neighborhoods have changed. The rules have changed. Halloween costs more and looks shinier. But the heart of it, that delicious shiver in the dark, the knock on the door, the kindness of a neighbor's smile, still belongs to children. We were those children once. And for one night every October, in a way, we still are.

So there they are, twenty-five things about Halloween in the sixties. The sheets and the boxed masks, the pillowcases and the porch lights, the popcorn balls and the orange boxes and the long walk home in the dark. It was a simpler, cheaper, freer night, and it was built by the people who raised us: the mothers who sewed and baked, the fathers who carved and answered the door, and the neighbors who made every child on the street feel welcome. Our generation was lucky to grow up that way, and those stories are worth passing on. Tell me in the comments your favorite Halloween memory, and what the best house on your street handed out.
