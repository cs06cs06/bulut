# Research: 15 Things in Every 1960s Kitchen That Have Vanished Forever

## Candidate items

1. **The wall phone with the long, stretched cord** — Hung by the kitchen doorway. The coiled cord was pulled across the room so Mom could stir the pot while she talked. You heard the rotary dial clicking back and a loud mechanical bell. Kids dragged the cord into the pantry for privacy, and it went kinky and tangled. | [VERIFIED] The Bell System tested push-button "touch-tone" phones in homes in Findlay, Ohio from Nov 1, 1960 and introduced them officially on Nov 18, 1963. Rotary dials stayed common in homes well after that (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-button_telephone). [VERIFIED] The small "Princess" phone came out in 1959. It was sold for the bedroom, had a dial that lit up as a night-light, and came in pink, turquoise, yellow, moss-green, beige and other colors (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_telephone). *Unverified:* most families rented their phone from the phone company rather than owning it until the late 1970s/early 1980s. *Unverified:* rural party lines were still common in the 1960s. | Today: cell phones, and fewer homes keep a landline. | US/Canada: Canada had Bell Canada plus provincial phone companies, especially on the Prairies *(unverified)*. | Image: a woman in an apron leans against the counter with a mint-green wall phone to her ear and the cord stretched to the stove. Late-afternoon window light.

2. **The percolator "blub-blub" on the counter** — A chrome or aluminum pot with a glass knob on top. You watched the coffee bubble up into the knob, darker and darker, and the smell filled the house before anyone was up. | [VERIFIED] Electric percolators existed by the 1900s (a 1905 pamphlet on "Coffee Making By Electricity"), and automatic ones by the 1940s. They were "supplanted in the early 1970s" by automatic drip machines (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_percolator). [VERIFIED] A US patent's background section says nearly all electric coffee makers sold in the US before 1972 were percolators, and that drip brewing took off from 1972 (US Patent 4,704,954, image-ppubs.uspto.gov). [VERIFIED] Pre-measured coffee "filter rings" for percolators came out in 1970 and were nearly gone by the end of that decade (Wikipedia). | Today: drip machines, single-serve pods, espresso. | US/Canada: similar in both. | Image: an early-morning kitchen with a chrome percolator on a Formica counter, coffee bubbling in the glass knob, steam, and a Dad's mug beside it.

3. **Avocado, harvest gold and coppertone appliances** — Fridge, stove and even the blender in one matching earthy color. (Many people remember harvest gold as "mustard".) | [VERIFIED] White appliances were the norm at first. The 1950s brought pastels such as yellow, pink, turquoise and blue. Coppertone appeared and stayed popular until the 1980s. In the '60s turquoise gave way to avocado and harvest gold, which lasted into the early 1980s. Almond took over at the end of the '70s, and white came back in the '80s–'90s (Star Tribune, startribune.com/appliance-colors-tell-kitchen-history/183530031). | Today: stainless steel and black. | US/Canada: same trend (unverified for Canada specifically). | Image: a full avocado-green kitchen with fridge, wall oven and matching canisters, a boy grabbing milk, warm tungsten light.

4. **Bagged milk and the pitcher (Canada) / the milk box on the porch (US & Canada)** — Canada: a floppy plastic bag set in a hard plastic pitcher, with the corner snipped off with scissors. Both countries: glass bottles left at the door at dawn, with cream risen to the top. | [VERIFIED] DuPont introduced plastic milk bags in 1967. They caught on in parts of Canada because 1970s metrication meant bottles, jugs and cartons had to be redesigned. Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes adopted them in the 1970s. Ontario's deposit rule on milk jugs, which didn't apply to bags, also helped. Canadian bags hold 1⅓ L (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_bag). *Unverified:* home milk delivery fell sharply in the US from the 1960s into the 1970s as supermarkets and cartons spread. | Today: delivery is gone almost everywhere; bags are still common in Ontario and Quebec. | US/Canada: **strong Canada-only angle.** | Image: a child snips a milk bag corner at a kitchen table, or a milkman with a wire carrier of glass bottles on a frosty porch.

5. **The bread box** — A metal or wooden box with a roll-top lid, often with "BREAD" painted on it, sitting next to the toaster. It smelled of fresh loaves and held cookies too. | *Unverified:* common through the 1950s–60s, and it faded as people refrigerated bread and kitchens got less cluttered. | Today: bread lives in a bag, the fridge or the freezer. | US/Canada: both. | Image: a chrome-and-copper bread box opened to show a sliced loaf, with a boy reaching in.

6. **The gelatin mold** — A wobbly ring of lime gelatin with fruit cocktail, cottage cheese or shredded carrot set inside. Copper or aluminum molds hung on the wall as decoration. It was turned out onto a plate of lettuce at every potluck. | *Unverified:* peaked in the 1950s–60s. | Today: mostly a joke or a nostalgia dish. | US/Canada: both. | Image: a woman flips a molded ring onto a glass platter at a holiday table while kids watch it jiggle.

7. **The "church key" and the can opener on the wall** — A flat steel opener in the drawer for punching triangles in juice and pop cans. A hand-crank opener screwed to the wall, and later a humming electric one. | *Unverified:* pull-tab cans spread from the early-to-mid 1960s, stay-on tabs came in the mid-1970s, and countertop electric can openers became popular in the mid-to-late 1950s. | Today: pull-tabs on almost everything. | US/Canada: both. | Image: a Dad punching a juice can with a church key, and a wall opener above the counter.

8. **Aluminum ice cube trays with the lever** — An ice-cold metal tray that stuck to your fingers. You yanked the lever and the cubes cracked and flew. The freezer compartment was small and frosted over. | *Unverified:* metal lever trays were common into the 1960s; plastic twist trays and automatic icemakers replaced them. | Today: ice makers and dispensers in the fridge door. | Image: a child at the sink yanking the lever, with frost smoke and cubes scattering.

9. **The grease can by the stove** — A coffee can or a special container on the back of the stove for saving bacon fat, used later for frying eggs and greasing pans. | *Unverified:* an era-wide habit with roots in Depression and wartime thrift. | Today: rare, as health advice and diets changed. | Image: a cast-iron skillet of bacon with a dented can beside it, and morning light through steam.

10. **The canister set** — Four nested canisters in a row: flour, sugar, coffee, tea. Tin, ceramic or copper-colored, matching the appliances. | *Unverified:* standard on 1950s–70s counters. | Today: pantry containers and clear plastic bins. | Image: a harvest-gold canister set on a counter beside a recipe box.

11. **The chrome-and-Formica dinette set** — A boomerang- or speckle-patterned laminate table with chrome legs and vinyl chairs that stuck to your legs in summer and split at the seams. | *Unverified:* the dominant 1950s–early 60s style. | Today: mostly wood or kitchen islands. | Image: a family breakfast at a red vinyl and chrome table.

12. **The waxed linoleum floor** — Patterned linoleum or vinyl tile, mopped and waxed on Saturdays. Kids slid on it in their socks. | *Unverified:* linoleum gave way to sheet vinyl through the 1960s–70s. | Today: tile, laminate, hardwood. | Image: Mom on her knees with a wax applicator, kids kept out in the doorway.

13. **The recipe box** — A tin box of stained, handwritten index cards. Grandma's handwriting, "from the kitchen of…" cards, clippings from magazines and community fundraiser cookbooks. | *Unverified as a statistic; a widely remembered habit.* | Today: recipes on phones and websites. **Strong for viewer comments and stories.** | Image: close-up of weathered hands flipping through floury cards.

14. **Hand tools: rotary egg beater and flour sifter** — The whir of the beater, the squeak of the sifter crank, flour dust on the counter. | *Unverified:* electric hand mixers spread through the 1950s–60s, but hand tools lasted. | Today: stand mixers and pre-sifted flour. | Image: a girl on a step stool turning the egg beater crank.

15. **The dish rack and the dish towel** — No dishwasher. One person washed and one dried, often siblings arguing at the sink after supper. | *Unverified:* only a small share of homes had dishwashers in the 1960s, and it rose through the 1970s. Exact percentages need a source (e.g., US Census or Department of Energy data). | Today: most homes have one. | Image: two kids at the sink, one with suds and one with a towel, the window dark outside.

16. **TV dinners and folding TV trays** — Aluminum compartment trays heated in the oven for about 25 minutes. Folding metal trays in front of the console TV. | *Unverified:* introduced in the early-to-mid 1950s and widespread in the 1960s. Microwave-safe trays replaced aluminum later. | Image: a family on the couch with TV trays and foil dinners, lit by the screen's glow.

17. **The ashtray on the kitchen table** — Glass or ceramic, next to the coffee cups and the sugar bowl. Smoke hung under the kitchen light. | *Unverified:* about 4 in 10 US adults smoked in the mid-1960s (CDC figures). | Today: smoking indoors is rare and banned in public places. *Handle gently.* | Image: adults at the table with coffee cups and an amber glass ashtray, the kids' schoolbooks nearby.

18. **The kitchen wall clock (sunburst or teapot shape)** — An electric clock with a soft hum, the second hand sweeping. Shaped like a teapot or a starburst above the stove. | *Unverified.* | Today: the time is on the microwave or a phone. | Image: a sunburst clock above an avocado stove.

19. **The deep freeze** — A chest freezer in the basement or on the porch, holding a side of beef, bagged garden corn and berries. Kids leaned in for a popsicle and felt the cold fog. | *Unverified:* home freezers spread through the 1950s–60s. | Today: bigger fridge freezers and frequent shopping. | Image: a boy half inside a chest freezer pulling out a box.

20. **The pressure cooker rattle** — The jiggling weight hissed and danced on the stove, and kids were told to stay back. | *Unverified.* | Today: electric multicookers. | Image: a pot with a dancing weight and steam, Mom watching the clock.

21. **Margarine color rules (US & Canada)** — Older viewers remember kneading a color capsule into white margarine. | *Unverified:* some US states kept color restrictions into the 1960s. Ontario allowed yellow margarine only in 1995 and Quebec in 2008. | **Good US/Canada angle, but needs a source before use.** | Image: a child squeezing a plastic bag of margarine until it turns yellow.

22. **Metric changeover in the Canadian kitchen** — Recipes in cups, groceries suddenly in kilograms and litres, measuring cups marked in both units. | [VERIFIED, indirectly] 1970s metrication in Canada forced milk containers to be redesigned (Milk bag, Wikipedia). *Unverified:* the timeline for grocery labeling. | Canada only. | Image: a mother squinting at a dual-unit measuring cup.

23. **The fondue pot** — Orange or avocado enamel pot, long forks with colored tips, cheese or oil night with the neighbors. | *Unverified:* a 1960s–70s trend in North America. | Today: an occasional novelty. | Image: a 1970s dinner party around a fondue pot, candlelight, orange hues.

24. **Stamp books in the kitchen drawer** — Saving grocery trading stamps and licking them into books at the kitchen table, then trading the books in for a toaster or lamp. | *Unverified:* peak in the 1960s, faded in the 1970s. Avoid brand names in the script. | Image: grandma and child licking stamps at the table.

25. **Nesting glass mixing bowls and patterned casserole dishes** — Primary-color glass bowls, cornflower-pattern white casseroles, the same dish every Sunday. | *Unverified; keep it generic, no brands.* | Today: these now sell as collectibles. | Image: a stack of colored glass bowls on a counter.

26. **Returnable glass pop bottles** — Bottles in the wooden crate by the back door, returned for a few cents each. 27. **The junk drawer and the free calendar** — String, rubber bands, birthday candles, a screwdriver. On the wall, a free calendar from the local bank or hardware store. | *Unverified (common habit).* | Image: an open drawer full of clutter, the calendar beside the phone.

28. **The arrival of the microwave (the end of an era)** — The first countertop microwaves were huge and expensive.
