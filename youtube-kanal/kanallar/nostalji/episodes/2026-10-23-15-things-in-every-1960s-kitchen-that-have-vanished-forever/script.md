# 15 Things in Mom's 1960s Kitchen You'll Never See Again

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Coffee perking before dawn. All gone now. Let's walk back into Mom's kitchen. And number one? It hung by the kitchen doorway in almost every home, and it gave many of us our first taste of privacy. I bet you can still hear it ring.

## #15 The Percolator Going Blub-Blub

**s02 · photos**

Before anyone else was up, you heard it. Blub. Blub-blub. The percolator on the counter, chrome and shiny, with that little glass knob on top. We watched the coffee jump up into that knob, a little darker every time. The smell crept down the hall and right into your bedroom. That was the alarm clock. Back then, nearly every electric coffee maker sold in the States was a percolator. Then, in the early seventies, drip machines took over, and the blub-blub went quiet. Today it's pods and buttons. Quick, yes. But no coffee maker ever said good morning quite like that one.

## #14 The Aluminum Ice Cube Tray

**s03 · photos**

Remember the metal ice cube tray? You pried it out of a tiny freezer compartment caked with frost. It was so cold it stuck to your fingers. Then came the lever. You yanked it up, there was a crack like a branch snapping, and cubes went skating across the counter and onto the floor. Somebody always had to rinse them off. And whoever emptied the tray was supposed to refill it. Nobody ever did. Today the fridge door drops ice into your glass, quiet as you please. Easier, sure. But a lot less exciting.

## #13 The Bread Box by the Toaster

**s04 · photos**

Next to the toaster sat the bread box. Metal or wood, with a roll-top lid that rumbled when you pushed it up. Inside, a soft sliced loaf, and if you were lucky, a few cookies Mom thought she'd hidden. Lift that lid and you got a puff of fresh bread smell. It sounds so simple. But in a lot of homes, that box was the heart of the counter. Somewhere along the way, bread moved into the fridge, the freezer, or just stayed in its bag. The bread box quietly disappeared. The cookies, of course, found new hiding places.

## #12 The Church Key and Wall Opener

**s05 · photos**

In the kitchen drawer lived a flat little piece of steel we called the church key. One end for bottle caps. The other end for cans. You punched one triangle to pour, and a second one on the other side so the juice wouldn't glug. On the wall, there was a crank can opener, screwed right into the cabinet. Turn, turn, turn, and the lid curled up. Later, in a lot of homes, an electric one hummed on the counter. Today almost every can has a pull-tab. Ask a grandchild to open a can with a church key. Then grab a towel.

## #11 The Matching Canister Set

**s06 · photos**

Lined up along the counter, biggest to smallest: flour, sugar, coffee, tea. The canister set. Tin, or ceramic, or copper-colored to match the stove. Mom never had to look. Her hand just knew where the sugar was. The flour one always had a little white dust around the lid, and the coffee one rattled when you shook it. For a lot of young couples setting up house, those four canisters were one of the first things they bought. Now it's clear plastic bins hiding in a pantry. Practical. But nobody ever lined them up with pride.

## #10 The Grease Can on the Stove

**s07 · photos**

On the back of the stove sat a dented old coffee can. Every morning, after the bacon, Mom tipped the skillet and poured the hot fat right in. Nothing went to waste. That grease fried tomorrow's eggs, greased the cake pan, and gave the green beans a flavor you still can't buy. A lot of our parents learned that habit during the Depression and the war, and they never let it go. Today, most of us would get a lecture from the doctor. But oh, those eggs. Crispy at the edges, and worth every bite.

## #9 Washing and Drying, Side by Side

**s08 · photos**

After supper, the kitchen turned into a little assembly line. In a lot of homes back then, the dishwasher had a name, and it was usually yours. One of us washed, elbow-deep in hot, soapy water. One of us dried, with a striped dish towel slung over a shoulder. The dish rack dripped by the window, and the window was black with night. We argued about who washed last time. We flicked towels. And somehow, we talked. Today a machine does it all, quietly, while everyone scatters. We got our evenings back. But we lost that talk at the sink. If this brought back a smile, subscribe, because every week we walk back into another corner of the life we grew up with.

## #8 The Chrome and Formica Table

**s09 · photos**

The kitchen table wasn't wood. It was Formica, speckled or patterned with little boomerangs, trimmed in a band of chrome. The chairs were chrome too, with shiny vinyl seats, red or yellow or turquoise. In summer, in shorts, you peeled your legs off that vinyl like a bandage. Eventually a seam split, and Dad patched it with tape. That table saw everything. Homework. Report cards. Birthday cakes. Long talks after bad days. Today, kitchens have islands and stools. Nice enough. But nobody ever stuck to a kitchen island.

## #7 The Deep Freeze in the Basement

**s10 · photos**

Down the basement stairs, humming in the corner, sat the deep freeze. A big white chest you could practically climb into. Lift that heavy lid and a cold fog rolled out over your arms. Inside, a side of beef wrapped in white paper, bags of garden corn, berries picked last July. And somewhere at the bottom, the popsicles. You leaned in up to your waist, and Mom hollered not to fall in. That freezer was a family's savings account. Today we shop twice a week and our freezers are half the size. Back then, we were ready for anything.

## #6 Milk Bottles on the Doorstep

**s11 · photos**

Before the sun was up, you heard the clink of glass on the porch. The milkman had come. Bottles left by the door, sometimes in an insulated milk box, cream risen thick at the top. On really cold mornings, that cream could freeze and lift the cap right up. In a lot of towns, home delivery faded as supermarkets and cartons spread. And here's one for our Canadian friends. In Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes, by the seventies, milk came in plastic bags. You set the bag in a pitcher and snipped the corner. Many Canadians still do. Folks in the States are still puzzled.

## #5 TV Dinners on Folding Trays

**s12 · photos**

Some nights were special. Mom slid those aluminum trays into the oven. Little compartments: turkey, mashed potatoes, peas, and a square of something that might have been cobbler. Then out came the folding TV trays, wobbly metal legs and all, set up right in front of the console set. We ate in the blue glow of the screen. The peas always rolled into the potatoes. And that foil was hot enough to brand you. These days, the trays are plastic and the microwave does it in minutes. But eating supper in front of the TV felt like breaking the rules.

## #4 Avocado and Harvest Gold Everything

**s13 · photos**

Then there was the color. Back in the fifties, kitchens had gone pastel: pink, yellow, turquoise. But as the sixties rolled on, Mom's kitchen turned earthy. Avocado green. Harvest gold, the one so many of us just call mustard. And coppertone, a warm brown with a shine. The fridge matched the stove, the stove matched the canisters, and sometimes even the blender joined in. Those colors hung on into the early eighties, until almond and then white took over. Today it's stainless steel. Sleek, I suppose. But when the whole kitchen matched, you knew somebody cared.

## #3 The Wobbling Gelatin Mold

**s14 · photos**

No potluck was complete without it. A shimmering ring of lime gelatin, with fruit cocktail floating inside like jewels in a paperweight. Sometimes cottage cheese. Sometimes, heaven help us, shredded carrot. Mom set it in the fridge overnight, then came the moment of truth. She dipped the mold in warm water, flipped it onto a plate of lettuce, and tapped. We held our breath. Then, plop. It slid out whole, and the whole table cheered as it jiggled. The copper mold went back up on the wall like a trophy. Today it's mostly a joke. But we all took a spoonful, didn't we?

## #2 Mom's Recipe Box

**s15 · photos**

On the counter, or up in the cupboard, sat a little tin box of index cards. Mom's recipe box. Every card had a story. Grandma's handwriting, slanted and careful. A card that said from the kitchen of Aunt Ruth. A clipping from a magazine. A page from the community fundraiser cookbook. The best ones were stained. A spot of vanilla. A thumbprint of flour. A splash of gravy right across the instructions. Today, recipes live on phones and websites. Handy. But a website never had your grandmother's handwriting. If you still have that box, hold onto it.

## #1 The Wall Phone with the Long Cord

**s16 · photos**

Number one hung by the kitchen doorway in almost every home on the block. The wall phone. Mint green, maybe harvest gold to match. When it rang, the whole house knew. You dialed with one finger and waited while the dial clicked back, every single number. Mom tucked it on her shoulder and stretched the coiled cord clear to the stove, stirring gravy while she talked. And us kids? We dragged that cord into the pantry and shut the door. Our very first taste of privacy. In the States, push-button phones arrived in nineteen sixty-three, but rotary dials hung on in kitchens for years. No ringtone ever sounded like that bell.
