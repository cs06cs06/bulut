1. **Stacking 45s on the record changer** — details: a tall center spindle holding a stack of singles. A small arm held the stack level. How many records fit in a stack (often said to be about 6–10) is **(unverified)**. | today: playlists and shuffle do the job. 2. **The little plastic "spider" 45 adapter** — details: 45s had a big center hole. To play one on a regular phonograph spindle you snapped in a yellow or red plastic insert, or used a metal snap-in. Kids lost them constantly and kept a jar of spares **(unverified)**. | facts: the wide hole was designed for the fast-drop 45 changer **(unverified)**. | today: almost forgotten. Vinyl buyers today mostly own LPs. | US/Canada: same in both countries **(unverified)**. | image idea: a close-up of a palm holding three colored adapters next to a 45 with a large hole; a wood-grain dresser top.

3. **Paying under a dollar for a hit single** — details: allowance money counted out at the record counter, a paper sleeve (sometimes a picture sleeve), and the "B-side" you only discovered at home. | facts: list prices were $0.89 for a 45 in 1955 and about $0.98 as the near-standard list price from 1958 to 1968. A 4-song EP was $1.49. Most US labels stopped making EPs by 1967 (ratherrarerecords.com, citing *Billboard*, Jan 15, 1955). People often remember the lower discount-store price rather than the list price (same source). | today: one song costs nothing on streaming, or about a dollar as a download. Physical singles are mostly gone. | US/Canada: Canadian shelf prices aren't documented here **(unverified)**. Don't assume they matched US prices. | image idea: a boy at a store counter sliding coins across glass; the clerk holding a 45 in a paper sleeve; bins of singles behind him.

4. **Choosing "Mono" or "Stereo" when buying an LP** — details: the same album came in two versions. The stereo copy cost more, and many families had no stereo player yet. Kids studied the cover photos and liner notes while the record played. | facts: about $3.98 for a mono LP and $4.98 for stereo from 1958 into 1968. The stereo price held into 1972 (ratherrarerecords.com). The first stereo LPs went on sale to the public in March 1958 (Wikipedia, "Stereophonic sound"). True stereo usually cost about $1 more than mono. "Fake stereo" reissues of mono recordings cost 50¢ more, at $4.49 (Wikipedia, "Duophonic"). The industry aimed to go all-stereo for albums by early 1968 (bsnpubs.com), and conventional mono LPs were essentially gone by 1968 (rarehistoricalphotos.com). | today: everything is stereo. "Mono" pressings are now collector items. | US/Canada: in both countries **(Canadian pricing unverified)**. | image idea: two copies of one album side by side in a record-store bin, one stamped "STEREO" in a bright band; a father comparing them.

5. **The family hi-fi console** — details: a long walnut cabinet that was really furniture. A lift-top lid, a radio dial, a record changer, record storage, and cloth-covered speakers. It sat under the family photos and the parents' records were kept in it. Kids were often told not to touch it **(unverified)**. | facts: a stereo system could cost up to twice as much as a mono one, because it needed doubled amplifiers and speakers (Wikipedia, "Stereophonic sound"). Original 1960s console prices aren't confirmed here **(unverified)**. Restored 1960s consoles are listed online today at roughly $1,500–$3,500 (current Etsy listings, so these are asking prices, not sales). | today: replaced by wireless speakers. Many consoles were thrown out and have come back as retro décor. | US/Canada: in Canada consoles were also sold through big department-store catalogs **(unverified)**. | image idea: a 1960s living room with a console against a paneled wall, lid open, a dad in a cardigan lowering the tonearm, a mom on a sofa with a plastic cover, lamplight.

6. **The transistor radio under the pillow** — details: a pocket radio in a leather case, tuned to the Top 40 station after lights-out. A single earphone, crackle between songs, and a fading battery **(earphone detail unverified)**. | facts: the first commercial transistor radio went on sale in October 1954 for $49.95. Japanese makers then drove prices down. One writer remembers that by the 1960s a set cost about $15 and was a common birthday or Christmas gift (tompolandwriter.substack.com, a personal memoir, so treat the price as approximate). The first model ran on an unusual 22.5-volt battery (collectorsweekly.com). Later sets mostly used 9-volt batteries **(unverified)**. | today: phones and earbuds do this. Bedtime listening is now podcasts or streaming. | US/Canada: shared experience. Canadian kids near the border often tuned in US stations **(unverified)**. | image idea: a dark bedroom, a boy under a quilt with a small radio's dial glowing, a thin earphone cord, moonlight through curtains.

7. **Transistor radios at the bus stop, the beach and the ballpark** — details: a crowd of kids around one radio while waiting for the school bus, all playing at "full throttle" (substack memoir). 8. **The jukebox at the diner or soda shop** — details: a chrome-and-glass machine with bubbling lights. You could watch the mechanism pick the record. A coin dropped into the slot, and two or three letter-and-number buttons chose the song. | facts: in their recollections, people remember a nickel per play before about 1950, then a dime or "three for a quarter" in the late '50s and '60s (datalounge.com and senior.culture forum memories, so prices varied by place). Diners rarely owned the jukebox. A separate operator placed it and serviced it. A new machine in the 1940s and '50s cost roughly $400–$1,200 (eathealthy365.com). | today: digital jukeboxes or none at all. Restaurants pay for background music services instead. | US/Canada: both. A Toronto forum poster remembers jukeboxes in all his favorite hangouts in 1963 (genealogy-specialists.com, a recollection). | image idea: a teen couple leaning over a lit jukebox in a chrome diner, a milkshake on the counter, neon reflected in the glass.

9. **The little song selector at every booth** — details: a small chrome box mounted on the wall at each booth or counter seat. 12. **FM grows up at the end of the decade** — details: older siblings discovered FM: long album tracks, quieter DJs, stereo sound.
