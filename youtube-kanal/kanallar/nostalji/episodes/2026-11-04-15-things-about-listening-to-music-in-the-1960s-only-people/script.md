# 15 Ways We Listened to Music in the 1960s That Kids Can't Imagine

Biçim: listicle · Araçlar: 

## Intro

**s01 · photos**

Plop. Click. Hiss. That was how the sixties sounded. Here are fifteen ways we listened. And number one? Almost all of us did it after Mom switched off the light, sure our parents never knew. Stay to the end to find out if they did.

## #15 Stacking 45s on the Record Changer

**s02 · photos**

Remember that tall spindle in the middle of the record player? You'd slide on a whole stack of forty-fives. A little arm rested on top to hold them level. Then the bottom record dropped. Plop. The tonearm swung over with a click, a soft hiss, and the song began. On a Saturday night, a teenager could load a whole party's worth and never leave the dance floor. The forty-five single had arrived in nineteen forty-nine, and by our time it was everywhere. Today, collectors wince at records dropping onto each other. Back then? That plop was the sound of fun.

## #14 A Hit Single for Under a Dollar

**s03 · photos**

Allowance day meant one thing. A trip to the record counter. From nineteen fifty-eight to nineteen sixty-eight, the list price of a forty-five was about ninety-eight cents, and plenty of us paid less at the discount store. You counted your coins out on the glass. The clerk slid the record into a paper sleeve. Then you got home and flipped it over. The B-side. Sometimes it was better than the hit. Today, one song costs nothing at all on a phone. But nothing felt quite like owning it, right there in your own two hands.

## #13 Mono or Stereo at the Store

**s04 · photos**

Here's a question kids today would never understand. Mono or stereo? The same album came in two versions. From nineteen fifty-eight into nineteen sixty-eight, a mono LP listed around three ninety-eight, and the stereo copy about a dollar more. Stereo records first went on sale in March of nineteen fifty-eight, and plenty of families had nothing to play them on yet. So you bought mono, sat cross-legged on the floor, and read every word of the liner notes while it played. By nineteen sixty-eight, mono was nearly gone. Now those old mono pressings are collector's treasures.

## #12 A Record Player in the Car

**s05 · photos**

Believe it or not, some cars had a record player. Right under the dashboard. A factory option for the nineteen fifty-six model year played special small records and cost about two hundred dollars. It was gone by nineteen fifty-nine. Then, around nineteen sixty and sixty-one, a new unit arrived that could play a stack of up to fourteen ordinary forty-fives. Imagine showing that off at the drive-in. Now imagine the first pothole. Yes, it skipped. On real roads, it skipped a lot. Today it sounds like a joke. Back then, it was the future, bump by bump.

## #11 The Glass Listening Booth

**s06 · photos**

Before you bought a record, you could try it first. Many music stores had little glass-walled booths. You stepped inside, the door closed, and the noise of the store just disappeared. Just you and the song, no headphones needed. If you were lucky, someone special squeezed in beside you, sharing one album cover. They were common in the fifties, and one or two lasted into the late sixties in some big-city stores. Now a preview is a tap on a screen. But a screen never gave you a little room all your own.

## #10 The Record Club by Mail

**s07 · photos**

Then there was the record club. It started in nineteen fifty-five, partly to reach families who lived far from any record store. You found the ad in a magazine, with rows of tiny album stamps. You licked them, stuck them on a card, and mailed it off. One old ad promised twelve records for two dollars and eighty-six cents. The catch? Every month, a featured selection showed up unless you mailed the card back in time. And somebody always forgot. Today we call it a subscription. Back then, it was a surprise in the mailbox, whether you wanted one or not.

## #9 Top Forty and the Fast-Talking DJ

**s08 · photos**

Top Forty radio was our heartbeat. Fast-talking disc jockeys, bright jingles, and the countdown we waited for all week. The chart grew out of what was selling as forty-fives and what was playing on the air. When the DJ said, here's a brand-new one, you turned it up and held your breath. In Canada, stations mostly leaned on songs that were already American hits. That changed in January of nineteen seventy-one, when AM stations had to play thirty percent Canadian music. Today, most AM dials are talk. But back then, that little band of static was pure magic. If this brought back a song you haven't hummed in years, subscribe, because every week we take another trip back to the sixties together.

## #8 Push Buttons on the AM Car Radio

**s09 · photos**

In the family car, it was AM, one speaker, and a row of chrome push buttons. Press one, and the pointer jumped across the dial with a solid clunk. Dad had his station. You had yours. Guess who won. Drive under a bridge, and the song vanished into static. Drive between towns, and it slowly faded away, so you hunted for a new one. As most of us remember it, FM in the car was a rare thing for much of the decade. Today, cars stream anything, anywhere. But we learned every song between towns by heart.

## #7 The Eight-Track Goes Ka-Chunk

**s10 · photos**

Then came the eight-track. It was introduced in nineteen sixty-five, and by nineteen sixty-six, a factory-installed player was an option on some new cars. Buyers even got a free demo tape. You pushed in that chunky cartridge, and it went ka-chunk. Then, right in the middle of your favorite song, it clunked again and switched tracks. The song faded out, and faded back in. We didn't mind. It was our own music on the open road, no DJ required. Today, an eight-track is a curiosity at a yard sale. Back then, it was freedom that went ka-chunk.

## #6 Taping Songs Off the Radio

**s11 · photos**

The little cassette crept in quietly. It reached the United States in nineteen sixty-four, mostly for voices and dictation. Prerecorded music cassettes followed in July of nineteen sixty-six. But the real fun was recording your own. You held the microphone up to the radio speaker. You shushed the whole house. Not now, Mom! And then, just as your favorite song started, the DJ talked right over the opening. Half our homemade tapes had a barking dog or a slamming screen door on them. Now a playlist takes seconds. Ours took patience, and a very quiet family.

## #5 FM Grows Up

**s12 · photos**

Near the end of the decade, older brothers and sisters found something new. FM. In nineteen sixty-four, regulators in the States adopted a rule that took effect in nineteen sixty-five. In cities over a hundred thousand people, a company owning both an AM and an FM station could only repeat half of its AM programming on FM. So FM needed something of its own. Longer album tracks. Quieter DJs. Stereo sound. AM was for singles. FM was for albums. You'd hear it seeping under your big brother's door, and suddenly you felt younger and older at once.

## #4 One Radio, a Whole Crowd

**s13 · photos**

Waiting for the school bus, somebody always had one. A small transistor radio, held up high, playing at full throttle. Everyone leaned in. Everyone sang along, badly. Then came the argument over which station. At the beach, the ballpark, the town pool, those little radios seemed to be everywhere. Music finally left the living room and went wherever we went. Today, everyone wears earbuds, and playing a radio out loud in public earns you a few sharp looks. Back then, one radio made a crowd. And the crowd was the best part.

## #3 The Jukebox at the Diner

**s14 · photos**

Walk into the diner, and there it stood. Chrome, glass, and bubbling lights. You dropped in your coin, pressed a letter and a number, and leaned close to watch the machine pick your record. In a lot of towns it was a dime a song, or three for a quarter. Some places even had a little selector at your booth, with metal pages you flipped between bites of your burger. And here's a secret. The diner usually didn't own it. An operator placed it and kept it running. Today, nobody picks the music in a restaurant. Back then, for one dime, the whole room heard your song.

## #2 The Family Hi-Fi Console

**s15 · photos**

In our living room, music lived inside the furniture. The hi-fi console. A long, polished wooden cabinet under the family photos. You lifted the lid, and there was the radio dial, the record changer, and Mom and Dad's records tucked inside. Cloth-covered speakers at each end. A stereo set could cost up to twice as much as a mono one, so a stereo console was a real point of pride. In a lot of homes, the rule was simple. Look, but don't touch. On Sunday afternoons, Dad lowered the needle, and the whole house went soft and warm. A wireless speaker does the job today. But it never felt like the heart of the home.

## #1 The Transistor Radio Under the Pillow

**s16 · photos**

And number one. The transistor radio under the pillow. The first ones went on sale in nineteen fifty-four for forty-nine dollars and ninety-five cents. By the sixties, many of us remember them closer to fifteen dollars, and it was the gift every kid wanted. After Mom switched off the light, you slid it under the pillow and turned the dial, slow. Late at night, AM signals traveled hundreds of miles. Suddenly, a big-city station from a faraway state or province was whispering in your ear. A crackle, a song, a fading battery. So, did our parents know? I'd bet most of them did. They heard that tinny music through the door. They just smiled, and let us have it.
